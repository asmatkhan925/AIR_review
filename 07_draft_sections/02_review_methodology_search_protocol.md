# 2. Review Methodology and Search Protocol

## 2.1 Review Design

This article is designed as a structured critical review with systematic mapping elements. This framing is deliberate. Low-resource ASR in the foundation-model era is not confined to one method family or one benchmark tradition; it spans resource creation, multilingual corpora, self-supervised and weakly supervised speech models, adaptation strategies, pseudo-labeling, knowledge distillation, evaluation protocols, robustness, multimodal ASR, and LLM-assisted correction or rescoring. A narrow method survey would miss the interactions among these layers, while a fully exhaustive systematic review would require a complete PRISMA-style retrieval and screening flow that is not claimed here.

The methodological goal is therefore twofold. First, the review records search sources, eligibility criteria, screening decisions, citation-verification status, and evidence-control files so that the review process remains auditable. Second, it synthesizes the literature into a foundation-model-era taxonomy, evidence-to-claim map, Core 60 citation backbone, cross-block synthesis layer, and future research agenda. The review is not organized as a paper-by-paper narrative. It is organized around evidence-controlled synthesis: what each source can support, how strongly it has been verified, which research question it informs, and where its limitations constrain the manuscript claim.

## 2.2 Scope and Research Questions

The review is guided by one main review question: how have foundation speech models changed low-resource ASR, and what resource, adaptation, evaluation, and robustness challenges still prevent reliable recognition for underrepresented languages? Six supporting research questions structure the review. RQ1 asks what low-resource means beyond limited labeled hours. RQ2 asks how self-supervised, weakly supervised, multilingual, and foundation speech models have changed low-resource ASR. RQ3 asks which data-centric strategies remain necessary in the foundation-model era. RQ4 asks which adaptation strategies are effective under which conditions. RQ5 asks how reliable pseudo-labeling and knowledge distillation are for low-resource ASR. RQ6 asks how low-resource ASR should be evaluated and extended toward robust, multimodal, and LLM-assisted systems.

These questions map directly onto the manuscript structure. RQ1 is addressed primarily in Sections 3, 5, and 6, where the review defines low-resource conditions and relates them to resources, benchmarks, and taxonomy. RQ2 is addressed in Sections 4 and 6 through the transition from hybrid and end-to-end systems to foundation speech models. RQ3 is addressed in Sections 5 and 7 through dataset, benchmark, normalization, filtering, augmentation, validation, and data-quality evidence. RQ4 is addressed in Section 8 through conditional adaptation strategies. RQ5 is addressed in Section 9 through pseudo-labeling, self-training, and knowledge distillation. RQ6 is addressed in Sections 10, 11, 12, and 13 through evaluation, reproducibility, robustness, multimodal ASR, LLM-assisted ASR, cross-block synthesis, and the future agenda.

## 2.3 Search Sources and Search Strategy

The search protocol combines database searches, venue- or source-specific searches, citation chaining, block-based targeted evidence expansion, and targeted primary-source verification. The local methodology file records the intended search sources as Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, ACL Anthology, ISCA Archive, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect, arXiv, and Papers With Code. The search log also records source-specific or cross-publisher rows for ACL Anthology, ISCA Archive, IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar, ACM/AAAI/Springer/Elsevier, NeurIPS Proceedings, PMLR, JMLR, OpenReview, arXiv, and citation chaining.

The search emphasis is foundation-model-era low-resource ASR. The inclusion criteria prioritize work from 2019-2026, especially 2022-2026 for recent foundation-model, SpeechLM, multimodal, and LLM-assisted directions. Older papers are retained when they are necessary for historical framing, under-resourced-language definitions, hybrid or end-to-end ASR foundations, benchmark history, major datasets, evaluation methodology, or foundational adaptation and supervision methods.

The search log currently records 66 search rows. This number should be interpreted as a repository traceability count, not as a total number of retrieved papers. Several rows are broad discovery or verification rows with fields such as "Not counted; broad discovery row" rather than exact retrieval totals. Accordingly, this review reports only what the repository supports: the search log documents the search sources, queries, filters, included target papers, and verification notes; it does not support a global claim about total retrieved records or a complete PRISMA-style identification count.

## 2.4 Eligibility and Scope Boundaries

The eligibility criteria are defined by the locked research questions and the inclusion/exclusion file. Papers are eligible when they address at least one of the following: low-resource or underrepresented-language ASR; multilingual or cross-lingual ASR transfer; self-supervised, weakly supervised, or foundation speech models for ASR; adaptation strategies such as fine-tuning, continued pretraining, domain adaptation, adapters, LoRA/QLoRA, prompt-based methods, or parameter-efficient tuning; pseudo-labeling, self-training, semi-supervised ASR, teacher-student learning, sequence-level KD, multi-teacher KD, confidence filtering, or iterative relabeling; ASR datasets, benchmarks, robustness tests, dialect-aware or domain-wise evaluation, fairness, reproducibility, or compute reporting; and multimodal, AVSR, SpeechLM, or LLM-assisted ASR relevant to low-resource or noisy conditions.

Peer-reviewed papers are prioritized for central claims. High-quality preprints and emerging work are allowed when they clarify current frontier directions, but they are classified conservatively as watchlist or future-facing evidence unless primary or peer-reviewed status is verified. Methodological or reporting papers outside ASR may be used only as background support for documentation, data statements, model cards, or compute transparency; they do not carry ASR performance claims.

Papers are excluded or deprioritized when they have no direct ASR relevance, focus only on high-resource ASR without transferable insight, lack sufficient technical detail for extraction, provide no empirical or conceptual research contribution, duplicate a later verified version, or study general multimodal learning without speech-recognition relevance. Work focused only on one language or thesis-specific material is not used to define the review's central scope unless it illustrates a broader low-resource ASR issue such as normalization, dialect imbalance, pseudo-label quality, noisy speech, or multimodal robustness. The review is therefore field-level rather than language- or thesis-centered.

## 2.5 Screening, Verification, and Evidence Classification

The repository uses a layered evidence-control workflow. Search activity is recorded in `02_literature_search/search_log.csv`. Initial screening decisions are recorded in `02_literature_search/screening_log.csv`, which currently contains 12 data rows. Papers are then organized into the seed registry in `05_synthesis_matrices/seed_paper_map.csv`, which currently contains 166 data rows across the evidence blocks. Citation metadata and source status are tracked in `03_references/citation_verification_log.csv`, which currently contains 167 data rows. BibTeX entries are managed in `03_references/references.bib`. Claims are connected to evidence in `05_synthesis_matrices/evidence_to_claim_matrix.csv`, and the Core 60 reference set is used as the main citation backbone for central manuscript claims. Block G then operates as the cross-block synthesis layer, linking taxonomy, core claims, research gaps, section mapping, and table/figure planning.

The verification labels determine how sources may be used. `Verified-primary-source` indicates that the source has been checked against a primary publisher, venue, DOI, proceedings, or official publication page and can carry central claims when substantively relevant. `Verified-secondary-source` indicates that the source is documented through reliable secondary metadata or an arXiv record with venue information but should be used with cautious wording unless the primary source is also verified. `Watchlist-ArXiv` marks emerging work that may inform future-facing discussion but should not be the sole support for a strong central claim. `Needs-primary-source-verification` marks entries that require further verification before being used confidently in manuscript prose. `Background-support` marks methodological or reporting sources that can support documentation or transparency practices but not direct ASR performance claims.

These labels are not cosmetic. They control the strength of claims. Verified-primary sources are preferred for central claims about models, datasets, benchmarks, adaptation, pseudo-labeling, evaluation, and robustness. Verified-secondary sources can support synthesis when their status is clearly bounded. Watchlist and arXiv-only sources are reserved for emerging directions such as SpeechLM systems, contextual ASR, hallucination risks, LLM-assisted correction, or rapidly moving 2025-2026 work. Background-support sources help specify reporting expectations. Language-specific and thesis-related material is treated as illustrative only and is not part of the central evidence backbone.

## 2.6 Data Extraction and Synthesis Matrices

Data extraction is distributed across matrices rather than stored in a single table. The seed paper map records paper IDs, titles, venues, categories, RQ links, taxonomy layers, roles, limitations, verification status, and intended use. The foundation model matrix compares model families by training type, language coverage, data scale, ASR capability, low-resource strengths, low-resource weaknesses, adaptation options, and limitations. The dataset and benchmark matrix records resources by language coverage, hours where available, speech type, domain, transcription quality, metadata, access, evaluation use, and limitations. The data-centric, adaptation, pseudo-labeling/KD, and evaluation/robustness matrices record strategy families, papers, model or resource conditions, evaluation use, strengths, limitations, verification status, and manuscript use.

The Core 60 reference set provides a higher-level citation-control layer with 60 selected references. The evidence-to-claim matrix links manuscript claims to supporting papers and limitations. Block G provides the cross-block synthesis controls: a taxonomy synthesis matrix, an eight-claim synthesis map, a twelve-gap research agenda matrix, a fourteen-section evidence map, and a thirteen-item table/figure plan. Across these files, extracted fields include such elements as paper ID, citation key, model or method family, dataset or benchmark, language coverage, adaptation or supervision strategy, evaluation protocol, claimed contribution, limitation, verification status, relevance to RQs, and intended manuscript section. The exact schema differs by matrix, so these fields should be read as a structured extraction pattern rather than as a single uniform data form.

## 2.7 Synthesis Method

The synthesis method is thematic and cross-block rather than paper-by-paper. First, evidence was organized into Blocks A-F, covering resource and benchmark anchors, foundation models, data-centric strategies, adaptation, pseudo-labeling/KD, and evaluation/robustness. Second, the Core 60 reference set was selected as the high-value citation backbone for central claims. Third, BibTeX and verification gaps were resolved or marked so that citation status could constrain manuscript wording. Fourth, Block G was constructed to integrate the earlier blocks into a six-layer taxonomy, core synthesis claims, research gaps, section-to-evidence mapping, and table/figure planning. Fifth, Sections 3-14 were drafted from the matrices and companion evidence notes. Sixth, traceability was preserved through draft-section evidence-note files and decision-log entries.

This process supports the review's central argument: foundation speech models change the starting point of low-resource ASR, but they do not remove the full reliability problem. The synthesis therefore focuses on cross-layer alignment among resource conditions, language conditions, model choice, adaptation strategy, supervision reliability, evaluation design, reproducibility, compute transparency, and robustness.

## 2.8 Methodological Limitations

The review is structured and evidence-controlled, but it should not be presented as a fully exhaustive systematic review. The repository does not support a complete PRISMA-style count of all records retrieved, screened, excluded, and included. Some search-log rows are broad discovery rows without exact retrieval counts. Language and database coverage may be uneven because the field spans speech, NLP, multimodal learning, benchmarks, and applied ASR venues. Some emerging 2025-2026 work is necessarily preprint- or watchlist-based, especially around SpeechLM systems, contextual ASR, LLM-assisted correction, and hallucination evaluation.

Verification status also varies. Some papers are verified from primary venue or publisher sources, while others remain verified only through secondary metadata or require primary-source verification. Benchmark and dataset reporting varies across studies, so not all matrices can extract the same metadata fields with equal completeness. The BibTeX file has known duplicate-title warnings, although duplicate citation keys are not present in the current checks. Older language-specific and thesis-related material exists in the repository as background or source material, but it does not define the review scope. These limitations are handled through explicit verification labels, Core 60 controls, watchlist restrictions, and traceability from search records to matrices and draft claims.

## 2.9 Section Takeaway

The methodology supports the review's evidence discipline. Central claims are tied to verified sources and synthesis matrices, while emerging directions are separated from established evidence through verification and watchlist labels. The result is a structured critical review with systematic mapping elements: transparent enough to support a review-journal argument, but cautious enough not to overstate exhaustiveness or certainty. Section 3 builds on this method by defining what makes ASR low-resource beyond limited labeled hours.
