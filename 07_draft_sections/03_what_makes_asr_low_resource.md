# 3. What Makes ASR Low-Resource?

## 3.1 Beyond Labeled Hours

Low-resource ASR is often described as a shortage of transcribed speech, but the definition is too narrow if it is reduced to labeled-hour counts. Labeled speech remains essential: it supports supervised adaptation, calibrates decoding behavior, and provides the development and test sets through which recognition quality is measured. Yet a dataset with a given number of hours can represent very different scientific conditions depending on who was recorded, how speech was elicited, how transcripts were normalized, whether the data reflect the intended deployment domain, and whether evaluation metadata expose dialect, channel, and speaker variation. In this review, low-resource ASR is therefore defined as a multidimensional reliability condition: the available resources are insufficiently complete, balanced, validated, or reproducible to support robust recognition for the target language community and use case.

Earlier low-resource ASR work already treated the problem as an interaction among acoustic resources, pronunciation resources, text resources, language modeling, and evaluation design rather than as a single threshold of labeled hours [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey; @gales2014_babel_project_research_cued]. Challenge-style evaluations reinforced the same point by making resource packs, constrained training data, and benchmark comparability part of the task definition [@peterson2022_openasr21]. More recent multilingual and inclusive benchmark work extends the argument: a language's presence in a benchmark does not by itself show that the benchmark captures the dialectal, orthographic, demographic, or domain conditions that determine reliable ASR [@chen2025mlsuperb2challenge].

The practical consequence is that "low-resource" should be read as a statement about evidence quality, not only data quantity. Ten hours of clean read speech with stable orthography and matched test data does not create the same modeling problem as ten hours of conversational, noisy, dialectally diverse speech with inconsistent transcripts. Likewise, a large multilingual corpus may still be low-resource for a particular variety, speaker group, script convention, or deployment domain if that condition is poorly represented. This is why Section 3 anchors the review before the model history begins: foundation speech models change what can be transferred into a low-resource setting, but they do not determine whether the target setting has enough reliable evidence for adaptation and evaluation.

## 3.2 Resource Conditions: Labeled, Unlabeled, Weakly Labeled, Pseudo-Labeled, Multilingual, and Multimodal

The resource layer in the six-layer taxonomy distinguishes labeled, unlabeled, weakly labeled, pseudo-labeled, multilingual, and multimodal resources because each resource type carries a different function and a different risk. Labeled speech supports supervised adaptation and evaluation, but it can be narrow, inconsistently transcribed, or weakly documented. Unlabeled speech supports self-supervised learning or continued pretraining, but it does not by itself provide task validation. Weakly labeled and web-mined speech can provide scale, but subtitles, crawled transcripts, segmentation, language identification, and source heterogeneity can introduce systematic noise. Pseudo-labeled speech expands supervision through teacher models, yet its value depends on teacher reliability, confidence filtering, calibration, and domain match. Multilingual corpora support transfer, but they can overrepresent high-resource or well-standardized languages while leaving target varieties thin. Multimodal and audio-visual resources can improve robustness under noise, but they introduce collection, alignment, privacy, and coverage constraints.

Large public datasets show why these distinctions matter. Common Voice and VoxPopuli expanded multilingual speech resources and made many cross-language experiments more feasible [@ardila2020commonvoice; @wang2021voxpopuli]. However, dataset size without validation, metadata, licensing, source information, and dialect coverage is not enough. Dataset-quality work makes this concern concrete by showing that multilingual speech resources can contain transcript, audio, silence, and planning problems that affect fairness and reproducibility [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. Thus, a corpus can be valuable and still be incomplete evidence for a low-resource ASR claim.

Foundation models sharpen rather than remove this resource problem. Self-supervised and multilingual models such as wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, WavLM, and XLS-R show how unlabeled and multilingual speech can be converted into transferable representations [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @hsu2021hubert; @chen2022wavlm; @babu22_interspeech]. Weakly supervised and massively multilingual systems such as Whisper and MMS further show that large-scale supervision can create strong starting points for recognition across many languages [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. But these models still require target-language evidence to reveal errors, validate transcript conventions, compare dialects and domains, and judge whether transfer is reliable. Foundation models move the bottleneck from initial model training toward data quality, adaptation, and evaluation; they do not turn incomplete local evidence into complete local reliability.

## 3.3 Language Conditions: Dialect, Orthography, Code-Switching, Morphology, and Script Complexity

Low-resource ASR is also shaped by language conditions that affect both modeling and scoring. Dialect variation changes acoustic patterns, pronunciations, vocabulary, and code choice within a named language. Orthographic inconsistency changes the relation between speech and written reference text. Code-switching changes tokenization, language modeling, and error interpretation. Morphology can create large surface-form variation from limited lexical material. Script complexity can make spacing, diacritics, punctuation, casing, and normalization choices part of the ASR task rather than a cosmetic post-processing step.

These language conditions matter because the model and the metric see them differently. A model may fail because a dialect, accent, or code-switched pattern is absent from training data. A score may be misleading because a reference transcript normalizes one spelling convention while the model outputs another plausible convention. Orthographic normalization and transcript comparability therefore remain central reporting issues: they can change WER, CER, and cross-study interpretation. This does not mean that one normalization rule is correct for all languages. It means that low-resource ASR studies must document how transcripts are prepared and how scoring treats language-specific variation.

Benchmark and dataset evidence supports this interpretation. IndicSUPERB and ML-SUPERB-style benchmarks show that language-family and regional coverage matter for multilingual evaluation [@javed2023indsuperb; @shi2023mlsuperb]. AfriSpeech-200 makes accent and domain metadata central to understanding performance across speaker populations [@olatunji2023_afrispeech200]. The ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge extends this logic by making language varieties and inclusive ASR benchmarking explicit evaluation concerns [@chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. Pashto can be used as a brief illustrative example of dialect variation, transcript normalization, and script consistency, but the field-level claim is broader: a language is low-resource when its available resources fail to represent the linguistic variation that recognition and scoring must handle.

## 3.4 Domain, Channel, and Benchmark Mismatch

Dataset presence is not the same as deployment readiness. Read speech, broadcast speech, telephone speech, meetings, clinical speech, classroom speech, conversational speech, and web audio differ in speaking style, channel, noise, overlap, segmentation, privacy constraints, and transcription convention. A system that performs well on a clean benchmark can fail when moved to noisy telephony, local broadcast, dialect-rich conversation, or far-field meeting speech. Low-resource ASR is therefore not only a question of whether a language appears in a corpus; it is also a question of whether the corpus represents the acoustic and social conditions under which the recognizer will be used.

This mismatch is visible across both challenge and corpus evidence. Babel and OpenASR-style tasks treated low-resource ASR as a constrained-data and benchmark-design problem, not merely as language identification followed by training [@gales2014_babel_project_research_cued; @peterson2022_openasr21]. Multilingual benchmarks such as FLEURS, XTREME-S, ML-SUPERB, and ML-SUPERB 2.0 improve comparability, but they cannot cover every deployment domain, channel, dialect, or orthographic condition [@conneau2022fleurs; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @shi2024mlsuperb2]. Corpus evidence points in the same direction: large public resources can improve coverage while still requiring source-aware validation and metadata to support reliable claims [@ardila2020commonvoice; @wang2021voxpopuli; @lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech].

Foundation-model-era weak supervision makes the distinction especially important. Web-scale audio and weak transcripts can produce powerful general recognizers, but weak supervision is not equivalent to validated target-domain evidence. Whisper and MMS are central examples because they change what low-resource work can start from, while still leaving open whether the target language variety, channel, transcript convention, and deployment domain are adequately represented [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. The evaluation consequence is clear: aggregate foundation-model results can hide per-language, dialect, and domain failures. The low-resource question is therefore not simply "Can a pretrained model recognize this language at all?" but "Can it be adapted and evaluated under the actual resource, language, domain, and channel constraints?"

## 3.5 Evaluation Implications

If low-resource ASR is multidimensional, evaluation must be multidimensional as well. WER and CER are necessary because they provide standard, interpretable summaries of recognition error. They are insufficient, however, when used as pooled scores without information about language variety, domain, transcript normalization, speaker population, robustness, reproducibility, and compute cost. Pooled WER/CER can hide the same weaknesses that define a low-resource setting.

Three implications follow. First, evaluation should report language-, dialect-, domain-, and source-aware results whenever metadata permit. ML-SUPERB, XTREME-S, FLEURS, IndicSUPERB, AfriSpeech-200, and ML-SUPERB 2.0 all contribute to the move toward broader and more explicit coverage [@shi2023mlsuperb; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @conneau2022fleurs; @javed2023indsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. Second, evaluation must document transcript normalization and data-quality controls. Without that documentation, differences in punctuation, spelling, diacritics, segmentation, or filtering can masquerade as differences in ASR quality [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. Third, evaluation should include reproducibility and cost conditions. Foundation-scale models may improve accuracy while making adaptation, inference, or reproduction difficult for the communities and laboratories that low-resource ASR is supposed to serve. SUPERB-style evaluation and efficiency-oriented benchmark work support treating cost and reproducibility as part of the evidence package rather than as afterthoughts [@yang2021superb; @feng2022superbslt].

These points do not reject WER or CER. They locate them inside a stronger reporting framework. A useful low-resource ASR evaluation should specify the resource condition, language condition, domain and channel condition, transcript-normalization protocol, adaptation budget, model version, decoding settings, and available demographic or dialect metadata. Without these details, a reported score may be numerically correct but scientifically weak.

## 3.6 Section Takeaway

This section answers RQ1 by defining low-resource ASR as a multidimensional reliability condition rather than as a simple shortage of labeled hours. The condition can involve scarce labeled speech, weak validation, noisy or weakly labeled resources, teacher-generated supervision, dialect and domain mismatch, orthographic inconsistency, code-switching, morphology, script complexity, fragile benchmarks, and limited compute or deployment capacity. Later sections formalize these interactions through the review's six-layer taxonomy: resource condition, language condition, model family, adaptation method, supervision strategy, and evaluation setting.

This definition also creates the bridge to RQ2 and Section 4. Foundation speech models change the starting point by making self-supervised learning, multilingual transfer, weak supervision, and speech-text modeling more powerful. They do not erase the resource, language, adaptation, supervision, and evaluation constraints that make ASR low-resource in the first place.
