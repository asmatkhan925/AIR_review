# 3. What Makes ASR Low-Resource?

## 3.1 Beyond Labeled Hours

Low-resource ASR is often introduced as a problem of too few transcribed hours, but that definition is too narrow for the foundation-model era. Labeled hours remain important because supervised ASR systems still need paired speech and text to learn target-language acoustics, pronunciations, writing conventions, and decoding behavior. However, the practical difficulty of low-resource ASR also depends on how those hours were collected, who they represent, how consistently they were transcribed, whether development and test sets expose the right variation, and whether the resulting benchmark can be compared across studies. The older low-resource literature already framed the problem as an interaction among language resources, acoustic resources, lexicons, pronunciation modeling, and evaluation design rather than a single data-count threshold [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey; @gales2014_babel_project_research_cued]. Contemporary shared tasks and benchmarks continue this view: OpenASR21, ML-SUPERB, ML-SUPERB 2.0, and inclusive benchmark extensions treat resource scarcity as a combination of data quantity, task design, language coverage, and evaluation protocol [@peterson2022_openasr21; @shi2023mlsuperb; @shi2024mlsuperb2; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge].

The central issue is that the same number of labeled hours can represent very different learning conditions. Ten hours of clean, read speech with consistent spelling, stable speaker demographics, and a carefully matched test set is not equivalent to ten hours of conversational, noisy, dialectally diverse speech with inconsistent transcription conventions. Nor is a language well served by a large pooled dataset if the target speakers, dialects, scripts, or domains that matter for deployment are weakly represented. Low-resource ASR should therefore be treated as a reliability condition: a setting is low-resource when the available speech, text, metadata, evaluation protocol, and computational infrastructure are insufficient to support robust recognition for the intended language community and use case.

This broader definition matters because foundation models can change the starting point without eliminating the resource problem. Self-supervised and weakly supervised models reduce the need to train from scratch and make transfer learning more practical, but they still depend on target-language adaptation data, language similarity, transcript conventions, and evaluation design [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. In other words, the question is no longer only "How many labeled hours are available?" It is also "What kind of resource condition does the system face, and which parts of that condition remain unsupported by pretraining?"

## 3.2 Resource Conditions: Labeled, Unlabeled, Weakly Labeled, Pseudo-Labeled, Multilingual, and Multimodal

The locked taxonomy for this review separates the resource layer into labeled, unlabeled, weakly labeled, pseudo-labeled, multilingual, and multimodal resources. This distinction is necessary because each resource type supports a different kind of ASR evidence. Labeled speech supports supervised adaptation and evaluation. Unlabeled speech supports self-supervised pretraining or continued pretraining. Weakly labeled speech and web-scale supervision can expand coverage but often introduce transcript noise, language identification errors, segmentation problems, and source bias. Pseudo-labeled speech increases the apparent amount of supervision, but its usefulness depends on teacher reliability, filtering, calibration, and the match between unlabeled speech and the target domain. Multilingual corpora support transfer, but their value depends on language-family coverage, balance across languages, and metadata. Multimodal resources can improve robustness under noise or ambiguity, but their coverage is usually narrower than audio-only corpora.

Large multilingual datasets illustrate both the promise and fragility of this resource layer. Common Voice, VoxPopuli, and FLEURS expanded multilingual coverage and made cross-language ASR comparison more feasible [@ardila2020commonvoice; @wang2021voxpopuli; @conneau2022fleurs]. At the same time, the Core 60 evidence layer flags dataset quality and sociolinguistic representativeness as first-order problems, not secondary cleanup tasks. Lau et al. show that public multilingual speech datasets can contain systematic transcript, audio, silence, and planning issues that affect fairness and reproducibility [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. This supports the claim that low-resource ASR is data-limited in a qualitative sense even when nominal hours are available.

Foundation speech models sharpen this point rather than making it obsolete. Models such as wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, WavLM, XLS-R, Whisper, MMS, and speech-text foundation systems show that unlabeled, multilingual, and weakly supervised data can be converted into transferable representations or strong zero-/few-shot baselines [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @hsu2021hubert; @chen2022wavlm; @babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms; @seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt]. But transfer does not turn every language into a high-resource language. The target language still needs enough validated speech and text to reveal errors, calibrate adaptation, normalize transcripts, evaluate dialects and domains, and test whether the model's apparent multilingual capability holds under local conditions. Low-resource ASR in the foundation-model era is therefore not the absence of all resources; it is the presence of incomplete, mismatched, noisy, or hard-to-validate resources.

## 3.3 Language Conditions: Dialect, Orthography, Code-Switching, Morphology, and Script Complexity

Language conditions determine whether available resources are usable and whether reported error rates mean what they appear to mean. Dialect variation can create large acoustic and lexical differences within the same named language. Orthographic inconsistency can make equivalent utterances appear different to a scoring script. Code-switching changes the language-modeling and tokenization problem. Morphologically rich languages can produce large surface-form variation from limited lexical roots. Script complexity and multiple writing conventions can make word boundaries, punctuation, casing, diacritics, and normalization choices part of the ASR task itself rather than minor preprocessing decisions.

These conditions are especially important for languages that lack standardized digital conventions or have strong regional variation. Pashto, for example, may be used as an illustrative case when discussing dialect variation, transcript normalization, and script consistency, but it should not organize this review. The field-level point is broader: a low-resource language is not only a language with few recordings, but a language whose available recordings and transcripts may not cover the sociolinguistic and orthographic variation needed for reliable ASR.

Benchmark evidence supports this broader view. IndicSUPERB and ML-SUPERB-style benchmarks show why language-family and regional coverage matter when evaluating multilingual models [@javed2023indsuperb; @shi2023mlsuperb]. AfriSpeech-200 shows why accent and domain metadata can be essential for understanding ASR performance across speaker populations rather than treating a language or region as homogeneous [@olatunji2023_afrispeech200]. The ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge extends this logic toward language varieties and dialects, making inclusion itself part of benchmark design [@chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. These sources support a key RQ1 claim: low-resource ASR must be defined through language coverage and variation, not only through aggregate hours.

Orthography and normalization are part of the same problem. Evaluation rows in the project evidence map warn that WER and CER are necessary but insufficient when transcript normalization, morphology, script choice, punctuation, and formatting can change measured error rates. This does not mean CER is universally superior to WER, or that normalization should erase meaningful language distinctions. It means that scoring choices must be documented and language-aware. For languages with non-Latin scripts, optional diacritics, unstable spacing, or multiple accepted spellings, an ASR system may be penalized or rewarded because of the evaluation pipeline as much as because of acoustic recognition. Section 5 and Section 10 can develop this issue in more detail, but Section 3 establishes it as part of the low-resource definition.

## 3.4 Domain, Channel, and Benchmark Mismatch

Low-resource conditions also arise from mismatch between available data and the target deployment setting. Read speech, broadcast speech, telephone speech, conversational speech, classroom speech, clinical speech, meeting speech, and web audio differ in channel, speaking style, background noise, speaker overlap, segmentation, and transcription convention. A model trained or adapted on one domain may fail in another even if the language label is the same. For this reason, low-resource ASR cannot be evaluated only by asking whether a language appears in a multilingual dataset; the review must ask whether the dataset matches the intended acoustic, social, and domain conditions.

Historical and recent challenge evidence makes this point concrete. Babel-style work and OpenASR-style challenges placed low-resource ASR in constrained-data and challenge settings, where the task was not merely to recognize a language but to cope with limited training material, language-pack design, and benchmark constraints [@gales2014_babel_project_research_cued; @peterson2022_openasr21]. Multilingual benchmarks such as FLEURS, XTREME-S, ML-SUPERB, and ML-SUPERB 2.0 improve comparability, but they cannot cover every deployment domain or every dialectal and channel condition [@conneau2022fleurs; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @shi2023mlsuperb; @shi2024mlsuperb2]. The data-centric evidence layer reaches the same conclusion from the corpus side: public corpora can expand coverage, but validation, metadata, and source heterogeneity remain necessary for reliable claims [@ardila2020commonvoice; @wang2021voxpopuli; @lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech].

Web-scale weak supervision introduces a related mismatch problem. Whisper demonstrated that large-scale weak supervision can produce a strong practical baseline and reset expectations for zero-shot and multilingual ASR [@pmlr-v202-radford23a]. MMS scaled language coverage further and made language coverage itself a central foundation-model variable [@pratap2024mms]. These systems are important because they change what a low-resource project can start from. Yet weak supervision is not the same as validated target-domain evidence. Web audio, subtitles, crawled transcripts, and multilingual weak labels may be useful for pretraining while still being mismatched to community recordings, noisy telephony, local broadcast, or dialect-rich conversational speech. The foundation-model era therefore shifts the low-resource question from "Can we train at all?" to "Can we adapt, validate, and evaluate under the actual domain and channel constraints?"

## 3.5 Evaluation Implications

If low-resource ASR is multidimensional, then evaluation must be multidimensional as well. Pooled WER or CER can be useful summary metrics, but they can hide the very failures that matter most: dialectal underperformance, accent bias, domain fragility, inconsistent transcript normalization, code-switching errors, demographic disparities, hallucinations, and compute-related deployment limits. A model can improve average WER while still failing the speakers or domains that motivated the low-resource system in the first place.

The Core 60 and Block F evidence support three evaluation implications. First, benchmarks should report language-, dialect-, domain-, and source-aware results whenever metadata permits. ML-SUPERB, XTREME-S, FLEURS, IndicSUPERB, AfriSpeech-200, and the ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge all contribute to this direction by making language coverage, language variety, or speaker/accent metadata more visible [@shi2023mlsuperb; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @conneau2022fleurs; @javed2023indsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. Second, evaluation must include data-quality and normalization transparency. Dataset audits and transcript-quality evidence show that comparable numbers are not necessarily comparable if the underlying audio, transcript conventions, validation, and filtering differ [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. Third, reproducibility and cost should be treated as evaluation conditions, not afterthoughts. SUPERB-style evaluation and compute-efficiency challenge evidence support reporting beyond accuracy, especially when large pretrained models create new trade-offs between performance, adaptation cost, and deployability [@yang2021superb; @feng2022superbslt].

These implications do not require rejecting WER or CER. They require interpreting them as part of a wider evidence package. For low-resource ASR, a useful evaluation report should specify the resource condition, the language condition, the domain and channel condition, the transcript normalization protocol, the adaptation budget, and the demographic or dialect metadata available for analysis. Without those details, a reported score may be technically correct but scientifically weak.

## 3.6 Section Takeaway

This section answers RQ1 by defining low-resource ASR as a multidimensional reliability condition rather than a simple shortage of labeled hours. A low-resource setting may involve scarce labeled data, but it may also involve weak validation, noisy or weakly labeled resources, dialect and domain mismatch, orthographic inconsistency, code-switching, morphology, script complexity, fragile benchmarks, and limited compute or deployment capacity. This definition sets up the next section: foundation speech models have changed the starting point by making transfer, weak supervision, and multilingual pretraining more powerful, but they do not remove the need for language-aware resources, adaptation, and evaluation.
