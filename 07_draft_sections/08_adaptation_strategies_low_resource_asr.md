# 8. Adaptation Strategies for Low-Resource ASR in the Foundation-Model Era

## 8.1 Why Adaptation Is Conditional

Sections 4, 6, and 7 establish why adaptation has become the central model-side problem in foundation-model-era low-resource ASR. Section 4 showed that modern ASR often begins from pretrained SSL, multilingual, weakly supervised, or speech-language models rather than from systems trained entirely from scratch. Section 6 placed adaptation as one layer in a broader taxonomy that also includes resources, language conditions, supervision, and evaluation. Section 7 showed that data quality and normalization determine whether adaptation evidence can be trusted. Together, these sections imply that the main question is not "which adaptation method is best?" The better question is: which adaptation method fits the available data, language condition, model family, compute budget, deployment domain, and evaluation target?

This conditional framing is necessary because adaptation methods solve different problems. Full fine-tuning can strongly specialize a model when labeled data and compute are available, but it can overfit and may damage existing multilingual capability. Continued pretraining can align representations with target-domain or target-language speech, but it depends on unlabeled data quality and compute. Parameter-efficient adaptation can reduce trainable parameters and support modular deployment, but strong general PEFT method papers are not automatically direct low-resource ASR evidence. Prompting and contextual biasing can adjust decoder behavior, but they are bounded and require careful evaluation for over-correction, leakage, and hallucination. Forgetting control matters when adaptation adds a new language to an existing multilingual system rather than building a single target-language model.

The section therefore answers RQ4 by organizing adaptation around conditions rather than rankings. Foundation models reshape the low-resource bottleneck by improving the starting point, but they do not remove the need to choose and evaluate adaptation methods under target-language data, domain mismatch, language relatedness, orthographic conventions, compute constraints, and forgetting risk [@conneau21_interspeech; @babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. The synthesis claim is deliberately conservative: no adaptation strategy should be recommended without specifying the data condition, model family, compute burden, and evaluation protocol.

## 8.2 Full Fine-Tuning and Multilingual Transfer

Full fine-tuning remains an important adaptation strategy. It is not obsolete simply because foundation models are available. When a pretrained model has useful acoustic or multilingual representations and a target-language labeled set exists, full fine-tuning can provide a strong supervised adaptation baseline. Cross-lingual representation learning with XLSR-style models and later XLS-R scaling show why multilingual SSL can improve the starting point for low-resource ASR, while still requiring downstream fine-tuning and target-language evaluation [@conneau21_interspeech; @babu22_interspeech]. Weakly supervised and massively multilingual models such as Whisper and MMS extend the same logic: pretrained systems can provide practical baselines and broad language coverage, but adaptation and evaluation remain language- and domain-sensitive [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms].

The benefit of full fine-tuning is direct specialization. It updates the model for the target data distribution, transcript conventions, acoustic conditions, and recognition objective. It can be appropriate when a small but reliable labeled set is available, when the target language is sufficiently represented or related to pretraining languages, and when compute permits end-to-end updating. It also provides a useful reference point for evaluating more constrained methods such as adapters or LoRA because it reveals what performance may be possible when more parameters are allowed to change.

The risks are equally important. Full fine-tuning can overfit scarce labeled data, especially when the training set is narrow in dialect, domain, speaker population, or orthography. It can be expensive for large foundation models and difficult to reproduce without reporting model versions, decoding settings, hyperparameters, and hardware. It may also create hidden aggregate failures: a model can improve the average score while degrading a particular language variety, domain, or speaker group. Benchmark evidence such as ML-SUPERB helps expose multilingual adaptation behavior, but its protocol also constrains what can be concluded [@shi2023mlsuperb]. Direct low-resource foundation-model evaluation, such as work on Kanyen'keha ASR, is useful because it shows that adaptation choices still matter in specific underrepresented-language settings, but single-language evidence should not be generalized as a universal recipe [@geng25c_interspeech].

## 8.3 Continued Pretraining and Target-Domain Adaptation

Continued pretraining is most relevant when labeled target data are scarce but unlabeled target-language, target-domain, or closely related speech is available. Rather than immediately fine-tuning on a small supervised set, the model is first exposed to additional speech that may better match the target acoustics, domain, speaker style, or language variety. This strategy fits the foundation-model era because many pretrained speech encoders were trained broadly, while a low-resource target setting may have distinctive domain and channel characteristics not captured well by the original pretraining mixture.

The advantage is representation alignment. Continued pretraining can move a model toward colloquial, domain-specific, or target-language speech before supervised adaptation. Evidence on continued pretraining for colloquial Finnish ASR provides a direct ASR anchor for this strategy [@getman24_interspeech]. Speech-language-model work also shows that staged and continual pretraining can be part of broader speech-text model development, although such evidence should be kept bounded when the system is not a pure low-resource ASR study [@tian25b_interspeech].

The limitations follow from the data-centric concerns in Section 7. Continued pretraining is only as useful as the unlabeled data are relevant, clean, and representative. If the additional speech is poorly segmented, mislabeled, domain-skewed, or orthographically mismatched when later paired with transcripts, the adaptation may reinforce the wrong conditions. It also requires compute and can over-specialize a model to one domain or variety. Continued pretraining should therefore be evaluated against supervised fine-tuning baselines, target-domain test sets, and, where possible, prior-language or prior-domain retention. It is best framed as a conditional strategy for unlabeled target-domain availability, not as a universal substitute for supervised adaptation.

## 8.4 Parameter-Efficient Adaptation: Adapters, LoRA, and QLoRA

Parameter-efficient adaptation is attractive for low-resource ASR because it promises lower trainable-parameter count, lower memory cost, modular language or domain specialization, easier storage of multiple target-language variants, and potentially simpler deployment under constrained resources. Adapters, LoRA, and QLoRA are the main method families currently represented in the repository evidence. Houlsby adapters, LoRA, and QLoRA are strong general method anchors for parameter-efficient transfer and memory-efficient fine-tuning [@pmlr-v97-houlsby19a; @hu2022lora; @dettmers2023qlora]. However, they should not be cited as proof that PEFT is best for low-resource ASR. They define mechanisms and efficiency motivations; they do not by themselves establish ASR-specific performance.

Direct ASR evidence should carry the stronger claims. Adapter pre-training and low-resource adapter tuning provide speech-specific support for adapter-based adaptation under low-resource and unseen-domain conditions [@udupa24_interspeech]. New-language adaptation and forgetting-control work provides another direct ASR anchor because it compares adaptation choices when adding a language to an existing ASR foundation model [@qian24_interspeech]. These sources allow the review to say that parameter-efficient methods are promising and practically important for low-resource ASR, especially under compute, memory, or modularity constraints. They do not justify a blanket claim that adapters, LoRA, or QLoRA are universally better than full fine-tuning.

The risk is overtransfer from NLP to ASR. ASR adaptation depends on acoustic representations, frame or token alignment, decoding behavior, transcript conventions, and evaluation metrics. A PEFT method that is efficient for language modeling may behave differently when attached to a speech encoder, encoder-decoder recognizer, CTC system, or speech-language model. Results may depend on layer placement, adapter size, rank, quantization, decoding settings, language coverage, and the amount and quality of adaptation data. The Section 8 position is therefore conservative: PEFT should be evaluated with ASR-specific baselines, matched data budgets, compute reporting, and forgetting tests rather than promoted as the default solution.

## 8.5 Related-Language, Phonetic, and Language-Code Adaptation

Related-language and phonetic adaptation are important because low-resource languages rarely exist in isolation. A target language may have related high-resource languages, shared phonetic patterns, overlapping scripts, multilingual speakers, or regional corpora that can support transfer. Cross-lingual SSL and multilingual foundation models make this transfer technically easier, but the transfer still depends on whether the donor language or pretraining mixture captures the target language's acoustic, phonological, lexical, script, and domain conditions.

Language-universal phonetic representation evidence supports the idea that phonetic-informed multilingual pretraining can improve low-resource speech recognition under appropriate conditions [@feng23_interspeech]. Related low-resource foundation-model evaluation also shows why donor and model choices must be tested rather than assumed [@geng25c_interspeech]. Language-code and prompt-style adaptation enter this subsection cautiously because they can provide lightweight ways to steer a multilingual model toward a target language or variety, but their reliability depends on the model architecture and evaluation protocol. New-language adaptation evidence shows that language-code tuning, prompt tuning, LoRA, regularization, and full fine-tuning can involve trade-offs between target-language gains and preservation of existing language capability [@qian24_interspeech].

The main risk is false relatedness. A donor language can be typologically or geographically related while still mismatching the target in phonology, dialect, orthography, or domain. Transfer can also encode biases from the donor corpus or overrepresent written conventions that do not match target speech. Related-language adaptation should therefore be evaluated with target-language dev/test data, language-aware normalization, and per-variety reporting where metadata permit. It should be presented as a strategy for exploiting plausible linguistic or resource proximity, not as a guarantee that related languages transfer cleanly.

## 8.6 Prompting, Contextual Biasing, and Decoder-Side Adaptation

Prompting, contextual biasing, and decoder-side adaptation are emerging or bounded strategies for foundation ASR and speech-language-model systems. They may be useful when the problem is not broad acoustic adaptation but domain vocabulary, named entities, terminology, code-switching cues, or recognition style. Whisper-like systems and speech-language models make these methods more visible because decoder behavior can sometimes be influenced without full model updating [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @tian25b_interspeech].

This evidence must be handled carefully. The repository treats many prompt-tuning and contextual-ASR papers as watchlist or emerging evidence, so they should not support strong central claims. The safe claim is narrower: decoder-side adaptation may help in settings where the model already has reasonable acoustic coverage but needs domain-specific vocabulary, context, or formatting constraints. It should be evaluated as constrained ASR support, not as free-form rewriting. The risk is that a contextual or generative component may over-correct, insert unsupported content, or improve surface metrics while harming faithfulness. Speech-to-text hallucination evidence reinforces the need to evaluate errors beyond pooled WER when generative behavior enters the ASR pipeline [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper].

For low-resource ASR, prompting and contextual biasing should therefore be treated as cautious additions to the adaptation toolkit. They can be useful for named entities or domain-specific terms, but they require source-grounded constraints, leakage checks, semantic preservation checks, and subgroup reporting. Section 11 will discuss LLM-assisted and contextual ASR risks more fully; Section 8 only positions decoder-side adaptation as a bounded method family whose maturity is lower than standard fine-tuning, continued pretraining, and direct ASR adapter evidence.

## 8.7 Forgetting, Reproducibility, and Compute Constraints

Forgetting and compute constraints turn adaptation from a single-target optimization problem into a multi-objective problem. Adding a low-resource language to a multilingual ASR model may improve the new target language while degrading existing language capability. Conversely, methods that preserve previous languages may sacrifice target-language specialization. New-language adaptation evidence makes this trade-off explicit and shows why forgetting control should be part of low-resource ASR evaluation rather than an afterthought [@qian24_interspeech].

Reproducibility is part of the same issue. Adaptation papers should report what was changed, how much data was used, how the data were filtered, which model version was adapted, which layers or parameters were trainable, which decoding settings were used, what hardware and memory were required, and whether inference cost changed. Without these details, readers cannot tell whether a gain comes from method choice, data quality, compute budget, decoding, or benchmark conditions. This is especially important for low-resource settings because under-resourced language communities and smaller research groups may not be able to reproduce expensive foundation-model adaptation.

Compute-aware reporting should include both training and deployment constraints. Parameter-efficient methods are attractive partly because they reduce adaptation burden, but the evidence base still needs matched comparisons and ASR-specific reporting. Toolkits and benchmark efforts support reproducible speech experimentation, yet the existence of a toolkit is not the same as a reproducible adaptation claim [@yang2021superb]. Robustness and challenge evidence similarly shows why decoding and scenario details matter when moving beyond clean benchmark conditions [@cornell2023chime7]. Section 8 therefore treats accuracy, compute, reproducibility, and forgetting as linked evaluation requirements. An adaptation method is not fully described unless the paper explains both the performance outcome and the cost and retention conditions under which it was obtained.

## 8.8 Adaptation Strategy Decision Matrix

The following decision framework summarizes how adaptation choice should follow from the low-resource condition:

- No labeled data, but a pretrained model is available: begin with zero-shot or diagnostic evaluation, then prioritize data collection, benchmark construction, or unlabeled-speech strategies before claiming target-language adaptation.
- Small labeled set available: use full fine-tuning as a strong baseline when compute permits; compare against PEFT when memory, modularity, or forgetting constraints matter.
- Substantial unlabeled target-language speech available: consider continued pretraining before or alongside supervised adaptation; evaluate against over-specialization and target-domain mismatch.
- Target domain or channel mismatch: adapt with target-domain speech when available, use robust evaluation by domain or channel, and avoid interpreting clean-speech gains as deployment readiness.
- Related high-resource donor language available: use multilingual transfer or phonetic-informed adaptation, but verify target-language benefit rather than assuming transfer from relatedness.
- Strong orthographic or transcript-normalization mismatch: stabilize normalization and scoring before comparing adaptation methods; otherwise gains may reflect text formatting rather than recognition.
- Strict compute or memory constraints: prioritize adapters, LoRA-style methods, or other PEFT strategies, but separate general method anchors from direct ASR evidence and report trainable parameters and memory.
- Need to add a new language without damaging existing languages: evaluate forgetting explicitly and compare full fine-tuning with parameter-efficient or regularized adaptation.
- Contextual vocabulary or named-entity problem: consider constrained prompting or contextual biasing only when leakage, over-correction, and semantic drift can be evaluated.

## 8.9 Section Takeaway

Adaptation is the model-side expression of the review's central argument: foundation models shift the low-resource ASR problem, but they do not eliminate it. Full fine-tuning, continued pretraining, PEFT, related-language transfer, prompting, and forgetting-aware adaptation each solve different problems and introduce different risks. The right adaptation strategy depends on labeled and unlabeled data, language relatedness, domain and channel match, transcript conventions, compute constraints, and evaluation design.

The next question is how to expand supervision when verified labels remain scarce. Section 9 therefore turns to pseudo-labeling and knowledge distillation. Those methods can enlarge the training signal, but they also risk amplifying teacher errors unless confidence, uncertainty, disagreement, and validation controls are made explicit.
