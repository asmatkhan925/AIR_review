# 11. Multimodal, AVSR, and LLM-Assisted ASR

## 11.1 Why Low-Resource ASR Is Moving Beyond Audio-Only Recognition

Sections 3-10 show that low-resource ASR is not only a shortage of labeled hours. It is also a problem of noisy or weakly labeled data, dialect and language mismatch, domain shift, orthographic inconsistency, fragile benchmarks, limited reproducibility, and compute constraints. Foundation models improve the starting point, but they do not remove these conditions. They often move the difficulty from basic model training toward language fit, adaptation design, supervision quality, and evaluation.

This is why multimodal and language-assisted directions have become important. Audio-visual speech recognition can use visual speech cues when the audio stream is degraded. Multilingual speech-text foundation models can connect ASR-adjacent tasks such as recognition and speech translation. Speech-language models can connect acoustic representations with text-side knowledge. LLM-assisted correction, rescoring, or contextualization can help with terminology, named entities, and formatting when the original ASR system already provides a constrained hypothesis space.

The central claim of this section is deliberately cautious. Multimodal, AVSR, SpeechLM, and LLM-assisted systems expand the design space for low-resource ASR, but they also expand the reliability problem. They introduce modality mismatch, visual data requirements, privacy concerns, hallucination, over-correction, prompt or context leakage, task-boundary confusion, compute opacity, and reproducibility challenges. This section therefore answers the future-facing part of RQ6 by asking not whether these methods are impressive in general, but when they are justified for low-resource ASR and how their risks should be evaluated.

The evidence base supports this balanced position. AV-HuBERT, robust self-supervised AVSR, and MuAViC provide multimodal and audio-visual anchors, with MuAViC giving verified multilingual audio-visual benchmark evidence [@shi2022avhubert; @shi2022robustavsr; @anwar23_interspeech]. SeamlessM4T and OpusLM show broader speech-text and speech-language-model directions, but translation-centric and early SpeechLM evidence should not be treated as direct proof that low-resource ASR is solved [@seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt; @tian25b_interspeech]. Hallucination-risk evidence and watchlist-bounded correction/contextual ASR sources show why Section 10's evaluation safeguards become even more important when recognition is connected to generative or contextual text-side components [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper; @ma2024asrerrorcorrection; @koudounas2025shallow; @wang2025contextasrbench].

## 11.2 Audio-Visual Speech Recognition and Multimodal Robustness

AVSR is best understood as a robustness strategy, not as a general solution to low-resource ASR. Audio-only recognition is vulnerable when speech is noisy, far-field, overlapped, reverberant, or recorded through poor channels. Visual speech cues can provide complementary information about articulation, especially when the acoustic signal is degraded. For this reason, multimodal representation learning and AVSR benchmarks are relevant to the evaluation and robustness layer of the review.

The repository evidence supports a cautious claim. AV-HuBERT provides an audio-visual self-supervised representation anchor, and robust self-supervised AVSR evidence supports the idea that visual streams can help under degraded audio conditions [@shi2022avhubert; @shi2022robustavsr]. MuAViC strengthens the evidence base because it provides multilingual audio-visual ASR and speech-translation benchmark evidence rather than only monolingual or narrow-domain AVSR background [@anwar23_interspeech]. Together, these sources justify including AVSR in the future-facing low-resource ASR agenda.

The limits are equally important. Low-resource AVSR requires synchronized video and audio, visible articulators, reliable speaker-video alignment, and enough language or variety coverage to support evaluation. These requirements can be harder than collecting audio alone. Visual data can introduce privacy and consent problems, cultural or contextual mismatch, camera and lighting bias, missing-modality failures, and accessibility concerns for speakers or settings where face video is unavailable or inappropriate. AVSR can also be brittle when lips are not visible, the speaker is off-camera, multiple speakers overlap, or the visual stream is desynchronized.

For low-resource ASR, the safe interpretation is therefore conditional. AVSR may be appropriate when the target deployment has reliable visual streams and the main difficulty is degraded audio. It is much less appropriate when privacy constraints, missing video, compute limits, or weak modality alignment dominate. Any AVSR claim should report audio-only, visual-only where relevant, and audio-visual results under both clean and degraded conditions. It should also state the language coverage, video domain, consent and privacy constraints, and missing-modality behavior. This directly follows the Section 10 principle that robustness claims require scenario-aware evaluation.

## 11.3 Multimodal Speech-Text and Speech-Translation Foundation Models

Multimodal speech-text foundation models broaden the space beyond conventional ASR. Instead of treating recognition as a single audio-to-transcript task, these systems may connect speech recognition, speech translation, text translation, speech-to-text generation, and multilingual representation learning. This matters for low-resource ASR because cross-lingual and cross-task supervision may provide stronger starting representations than a small target-language ASR corpus alone.

SeamlessM4T is the main Core 60 anchor for this direction. It is useful because it frames speech and text tasks jointly across many languages and connects ASR-adjacent capability with speech translation and multilingual speech-text modeling [@seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt]. Whisper and MMS provide broader foundation-model context: weakly supervised multilingual ASR and massively multilingual speech technology can improve starting points and coverage, but uneven low-resource behavior, normalization, domain mismatch, and evaluation comparability still require careful control [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms].

The task boundary is the critical issue. Speech translation, ASR, and post-ASR correction are not interchangeable. A model that performs well on speech translation may not provide the same evidence for faithful source-language transcription. A model that produces fluent translated or normalized text may be useful for communication, but it is not necessarily preserving the speaker's words, dialect forms, code-switching, or orthographic conventions. For a review of low-resource ASR, multimodal speech-text systems should therefore be used as evidence that the model space is expanding, not as evidence that ASR-specific resource and evaluation problems have disappeared.

The evaluation implication is direct. When a system combines ASR, speech translation, and text generation, the report should identify which task is being evaluated, what output representation is expected, whether transcript fidelity or translation adequacy is the target, and how language-wise and domain-wise errors are measured. Low-resource ASR claims should be grounded in ASR-specific evaluation or clearly labeled as ASR-adjacent transfer evidence. This keeps the Section 11 argument aligned with C-G7: these systems are promising, but their promise depends on evaluation boundaries and reliability controls.

## 11.4 Speech-Language Models and Speech-LLM Systems

Speech-language models and Speech-LLM systems connect acoustic representations with textual knowledge, instruction-like behavior, or broader language-model objectives. They may support ASR, speech understanding, speech translation, correction, rescoring, contextualization, or multitask generation. Their attraction for low-resource ASR is clear: if a system can combine speech evidence with strong text-side knowledge, it may help when direct labeled speech is scarce or when domain terminology is difficult.

The evidence should be handled conservatively. SpeechLM provides a speech-text pretraining anchor, but the Core 60 status marks it as verified secondary with primary source pending, so it should support cautious model-family discussion rather than strong low-resource ASR claims [@zhang2024_speechlm]. OpusLM provides a verified primary-source open SpeechLM anchor and is useful for discussing staged or unified speech-language modeling [@tian25b_interspeech]. These sources show the direction of the field, but they do not justify a blanket claim that SpeechLM systems reliably outperform ASR-specialized foundation models for low-resource languages.

The main reliability constraints are familiar from earlier sections. Speech-language systems still depend on language coverage, supervision quality, data provenance, adaptation method, decoding behavior, orthographic conventions, benchmark design, and compute. They can inherit errors from speech encoders, impose text-side regularities that do not match the target language, or generate fluent output that hides recognition uncertainty. Their evaluation may also mix ASR, speech understanding, translation, and dialogue tasks. If those tasks are not separated, a reader cannot know whether the reported gain is acoustic recognition, language-model completion, semantic understanding, or post-hoc rewriting.

For low-resource ASR, SpeechLM and Speech-LLM systems should therefore be evaluated as bounded system designs. Reports should specify the speech encoder, language model, adapter or connector, training data, task prompt where relevant, decoding constraints, supported languages, compute cost, and whether the output is intended to be a transcript, translation, summary, or corrected text. Without these details, SpeechLM evidence risks becoming a label for many different systems rather than a reliable ASR category.

## 11.5 LLM-Assisted ASR Correction, Rescoring, and Contextualization

LLM-assisted ASR should be treated as correction, rescoring, contextualization, or normalization support, not as a guaranteed replacement for acoustic recognition. The most defensible uses are constrained. An LLM can rescore N-best hypotheses or lattices, correct an ASR output when alternatives and confidence information are available, support named entities or domain terminology, provide contextual biasing, assist formatting, or normalize transcripts under explicit rules. In each case, the LLM should be bounded by acoustic evidence, ASR hypotheses, confidence scores, or validated context.

This distinction matters because unconstrained rewriting can make the transcript look better while becoming less faithful to the audio. A language model may replace a rare name with a common one, normalize a dialect form into a standard form, insert contextually plausible content, or change a speaker's intended meaning. Surface WER may improve under some scoring conventions while semantic preservation or source grounding worsens. Section 10 already established that hallucination, over-correction, semantic drift, and context leakage require evaluation beyond aggregate WER. Section 11 applies that requirement to LLM-assisted systems.

The local evidence supports cautious future-facing discussion, not strong universal claims. Careless Whisper provides the main Core 60 hallucination-risk anchor for the need to evaluate unsupported generated content [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper]. Watchlist evidence on LLM-based ASR error correction, hallucination benchmarks, and contextual ASR can motivate emerging protocols, but it should not carry central claims because these sources are marked as arXiv watchlist or current frontier evidence in the repository [@ma2024asrerrorcorrection; @koudounas2025shallow; @wang2025contextasrbench]. They are useful for identifying risks and evaluation needs, not for declaring that LLM correction is mature for low-resource ASR.

The practical rule is that correction must be traceable. If a system uses N-best lists or lattices, the report should show whether the final output remains within or near the acoustic hypothesis space. If it uses context, the report should specify what context was available, whether it could leak the answer, and how named-entity or terminology gains were evaluated. If it performs normalization, the report should distinguish formatting improvement from recognition improvement. If it corrects without acoustic constraints, the output should be treated as language-model rewriting unless evidence shows source grounding.

## 11.6 Reliability Risks: Hallucination, Over-Correction, Modality Mismatch, and Context Leakage

Reliability risk is the analytical core of Section 11. Multimodal and LLM-assisted systems are attractive because they add information channels beyond audio-only recognition. That same expansion creates new failure modes. A visual stream can help when audio is degraded, but it can also be missing, misaligned, privacy-sensitive, or mismatched to the target domain. A language model can help with terminology, but it can also hallucinate, over-correct, or hide uncertainty behind fluent text. A speech-text foundation model can connect tasks, but it can also blur the boundary between transcription, translation, correction, and generation.

The first risk is hallucinated content unsupported by the audio. This is especially important for generative ASR and LLM-assisted correction because fluent output can appear credible even when it is not source-grounded. The second risk is over-correction: valid speaker output, dialect forms, code-switching, hesitations, disfluencies, or non-standard orthography may be rewritten into a more standard but less faithful transcript. The third risk is normalization drift, where casing, punctuation, spelling, script, or formatting changes alter what is being evaluated. These risks are not merely textual. They affect whether a low-resource community's speech is represented accurately.

The fourth risk is hidden bias. Demographic, dialect, language, and domain weaknesses may be less visible when the final output is polished by a strong text model. The fifth risk is modality mismatch. AVSR may fail when visual data are unavailable, low-quality, culturally inappropriate to collect, or inconsistent with the audio. The sixth risk is context leakage: a contextual ASR or correction system may appear to recognize a named entity because the prompt, retrieval source, or metadata already contained it. The seventh risk is compute opacity. Large multimodal or SpeechLM systems may be difficult to reproduce or deploy in the settings that motivate low-resource ASR.

These risks connect directly to Section 10's evaluation framework. Multimodal and LLM-assisted systems should report audio-only, visual-only where meaningful, and fused results; missing-modality tests; noise and domain breakdowns; language and dialect breakdowns; normalization policies; hallucination and semantic-drift checks; context-leakage controls; compute and inference cost; and reproducibility artifacts where possible. The point is not to reject multimodal or LLM-assisted ASR. The point is to prevent a new layer of technology from hiding the same low-resource reliability problems the review has traced across resources, language, adaptation, supervision, and evaluation.

## 11.7 Multimodal and LLM-Assisted ASR Decision Matrix

The following decision framework summarizes when an AVSR, SpeechLM, LLM-assisted, or no-extra-layer approach is most defensible:

- Noisy or far-field audio with reliable visual streams: consider AVSR or multimodal fusion, but require audio-only, visual-only where relevant, and audio-visual results under clean and degraded conditions.
- Multilingual speech/text transfer need: consider multilingual speech-text foundation models, but separate ASR, speech translation, and post-ASR correction evidence.
- Low-resource ASR with weak orthographic conventions: prioritize transcript policy and normalization controls before using LLM correction; evaluate raw and normalized outputs separately where feasible.
- Domain-specific named entities or terminology: consider contextual biasing, constrained rescoring, or correction with explicit context, but test for leakage and named-entity hallucination.
- Correction or rescoring with N-best or lattice constraints: use LLM assistance as a bounded post-ASR layer and report whether the final output remains grounded in acoustic hypotheses.
- Correction without acoustic constraints: treat the output as high-risk rewriting and avoid claiming ASR improvement unless source grounding and semantic preservation are evaluated.
- Missing or unreliable visual modality: prefer audio-only robustness, adaptation, or data-centric improvements rather than AVSR dependence.
- Strict privacy or deployment constraints: avoid visual data collection or large external LLM calls unless consent, storage, and deployment risks are explicitly controlled.
- Compute-limited deployment: prefer smaller adapted or distilled ASR systems, PEFT, or bounded rescoring over large multimodal or SpeechLM systems unless cost is justified and reported.
- Hallucination-sensitive applications: require conservative decoding, source-grounding checks, human review where appropriate, and explicit hallucination, over-correction, and semantic-drift evaluation.

## 11.8 Section Takeaway

Multimodal, AVSR, SpeechLM, and LLM-assisted ASR extend the low-resource ASR design space. They can provide useful visual cues, multilingual speech-text transfer, stronger text-side priors, contextual terminology support, and constrained post-ASR correction. But their value depends on conditions: available modalities, language coverage, data quality, task definition, adaptation method, supervision source, evaluation protocol, reproducibility, compute, privacy, and source grounding.

The section's main conclusion is therefore not that the future of low-resource ASR is simply multimodal or LLM-assisted. It is that these directions must be integrated into the same evidence chain that governs the rest of the review. Section 12 should synthesize them with the resource, language, model, adaptation, supervision, evaluation, reproducibility, and compute constraints developed across Sections 3-10. Without that integration, multimodal and LLM-assisted systems risk becoming new sources of hidden error rather than reliable paths toward broader low-resource ASR coverage.
