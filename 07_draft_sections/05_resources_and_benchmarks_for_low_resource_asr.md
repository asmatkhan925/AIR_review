# 5. Resources and Benchmarks for Low-Resource ASR

## 5.1 Why Resources Still Matter After Foundation Models

Sections 3 and 4 established two linked claims: low-resource ASR is a multidimensional reliability condition, and foundation models have changed the starting point without removing the underlying resource problem. Section 5 turns that argument toward resources and benchmarks. Strong pretrained models make resource quality more important, not less important, because a better starting model can make weak benchmarks look more conclusive than they are. If a foundation model performs well on an aggregate test set, the result is only meaningful if the dataset captures the relevant language varieties, transcript conventions, domains, channels, speaker populations, and evaluation protocols.

This is the central resource consequence of the foundation-model era. Earlier low-resource ASR had to build many elements from the ground up: transcribed speech, lexicons, language models, and benchmark splits [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey; @gales2014_babel_project_research_cued]. Modern systems can often begin from multilingual or weakly supervised pretrained models, but those models still need reliable evidence that they work for the intended language and setting. Resource and benchmark design therefore become the mechanism through which claims about foundation-model progress are tested rather than assumed.

The synthesis developed in this review captures this through three linked claims. First, low-resource ASR cannot be reduced to labeled hours. Second, foundation models shift the bottleneck toward data quality, adaptation, and evaluation. Third, data-centric strategies remain necessary because corpus quality, filtering, normalization, metadata, and validation shape downstream model behavior. In practical terms, a resource is not valuable only because it is large. It is valuable when its provenance, supervision type, metadata, validation procedure, access conditions, and evaluation role are clear enough to support interpretable ASR claims.

## 5.2 Types of ASR Resources

ASR resources differ by what they enable and by the risks they introduce. Labeled corpora remain the most direct evidence for supervised adaptation and evaluation. They provide the paired speech and text needed to tune models and measure recognition quality, but they can be small, domain-narrow, dialectally skewed, or inconsistently normalized. Unlabeled corpora support self-supervised learning and continued pretraining; they are especially important after wav2vec-style and multilingual SSL models, but they cannot validate recognition behavior without downstream references. Weakly labeled and web-mined data can provide scale, but source noise, segmentation errors, transcript mismatch, and language-identification errors can shape what the model learns.

Pseudo-labeled resources occupy a middle position. They can expand supervision when human transcription is scarce, but they inherit teacher-model errors and require filtering, calibration, and validation. Multilingual resources support cross-lingual transfer and benchmark comparison, but they can hide severe imbalance if languages, scripts, domains, and speaker communities are unevenly represented. Multimodal and audio-visual resources can support robustness under noise or ambiguity, but they require aligned modalities and are often narrower in language and domain coverage than audio-only corpora.

The important distinction is that these resource types are not interchangeable. A large unlabeled corpus does not replace a small but carefully validated test set. A multilingual benchmark does not replace dialect or domain coverage. A weakly supervised dataset does not replace documentation of transcript quality. A pseudo-labeled corpus does not become equivalent to human-labeled supervision simply because it is large. Section 5 therefore treats resources as evidence instruments: each resource type enables a different form of model development or evaluation, and each must be interpreted through its own reliability risks.

## 5.3 Major Dataset and Benchmark Families

The repository evidence contains several dataset and benchmark families that illustrate how resource design shapes low-resource ASR claims. Common Voice is a major crowdsourced multilingual speech resource and is important because it links openness, community contribution, validation, and multilingual coverage [@ardila2020commonvoice]. Babel and OpenASR-style resources represent a different tradition: constrained low-resource challenge settings where language packs, limited training material, and comparable evaluation protocols are part of the research design [@gales2014_babel_project_research_cued; @peterson2022_openasr21]. MLS and VoxPopuli expand multilingual speech resources through large-scale corpus construction and unlabeled or semi-supervised use cases, but their domains and supervision conditions still shape what they can prove [@pratap20_interspeech; @wang2021voxpopuli].

Benchmark families such as FLEURS, XTREME-S, ML-SUPERB, ML-SUPERB 2.0, and the ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge extend the comparison problem from individual corpora to multilingual evaluation protocols [@conneau2022fleurs; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @shi2023mlsuperb; @shi2024mlsuperb2; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. Their contribution is not only that they cover many languages. It is that they make multilingual evaluation more systematic by organizing tasks, splits, model constraints, and reporting expectations. At the same time, these benchmarks also show the limits of coverage-based reasoning: broad language coverage does not guarantee dialect coverage, domain coverage, script comparability, or deployment readiness.

Other benchmarks foreground specific forms of representativeness. IndicSUPERB makes regional and language-family evaluation more visible for Indian languages [@javed2023indsuperb]. AfriSpeech-200 is important because accent and domain metadata become part of the dataset's value rather than optional description [@olatunji2023_afrispeech200]. Foundation-model-era data-pipeline work adds another dimension by showing that heterogeneous data sources, filtering, punctuation, and truecasing decisions can affect speech-to-text model behavior [@tian24_interspeech]. These examples should not be read as a catalog of datasets. They show that resource families differ in what they make measurable: openness, low-resource challenge design, multilingual transfer, accent metadata, domain robustness, or data-pipeline quality.

## 5.4 Data Quality, Validation, Metadata, and Documentation

Dataset size and language count are weak evidence if they are not accompanied by quality controls. Low-resource ASR often depends on corpora assembled from community contribution, public media, parliamentary speech, audiobooks, clinical speech, web audio, or challenge packs. These sources differ in recording conditions, speaker demographics, transcript conventions, licensing, and validation. A dataset with many hours may still be fragile if the transcripts are noisy, the speaker metadata are thin, the dialect distribution is unknown, or the validation process is inconsistent.

The multilingual dataset audit evidence is important because it moves this issue from general concern to concrete ASR risk. Lau et al. show that data quality issues in multilingual speech datasets can involve transcript problems, audio problems, silence, and planning failures, with implications for sociolinguistic awareness and proactive language planning [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. This supports the Section 5 claim that quality and documentation are not downstream cleanup. They are part of the evidence base that determines whether a model result can be interpreted reliably.

Documentation frameworks such as Datasheets for Datasets, Data Statements, and Model Cards are useful methodological support for this agenda. They should not be presented as ASR evidence, and they are not cited here because the current manuscript bibliography does not include them as citation keys. Their value is conceptual: low-resource ASR needs resource reports that document data provenance, intended use, collection process, speaker and dialect metadata, validation, licensing, and known limitations. That documentation is especially important when foundation models are evaluated across languages and domains that were not equally represented during pretraining.

## 5.5 Benchmark Comparability and Evaluation Risk

Benchmark comparability is difficult because ASR datasets differ along many axes at once. Two benchmarks may report WER or CER, but use different domains, scripts, tokenization conventions, punctuation rules, casing, normalization pipelines, train/dev/test splits, or speaker metadata. In low-resource ASR, these differences are not minor implementation details. They can change whether a reported gain reflects better recognition, better normalization alignment, easier domain conditions, or more favorable language coverage.

This is why multilingual benchmarks must be interpreted alongside their limitations. FLEURS, XTREME-S, and ML-SUPERB improve cross-language comparison, but they cannot substitute for dialect-wise, domain-wise, and source-aware evaluation in every deployment setting [@conneau2022fleurs; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @shi2023mlsuperb]. Orthography-aware evaluation evidence makes the same point at the scoring level: transcript conventions and naturally occurring spelling variation can change measured error rates, so normalization rules must be explicit [@karita2023lenient]. Robustness benchmarks such as CHiME-7 further show that clean read-speech evaluation does not cover far-field, multi-device, meeting, or overlap conditions that matter in deployed systems [@cornell2023chime7].

The foundation-model era increases this risk because stronger models encourage broader comparisons. A multilingual or weakly supervised model can be run on many languages, but the resulting table may hide weak performance on particular varieties, domains, scripts, or speaker groups. Benchmark comparison should therefore report not only aggregate accuracy, but also the resource condition, validation status, metadata availability, normalization protocol, model and decoding setup, and compute or deployment constraints. Section 10 will return to these issues in detail; Section 5 establishes that evaluation risk begins with how resources and benchmarks are constructed.

## 5.6 Section Takeaway

Resources and benchmarks remain central because they define what low-resource ASR progress can mean. Foundation models provide stronger starting points, but they do not validate the target language, document dialect coverage, fix transcript normalization, guarantee domain robustness, or make benchmark scores comparable by themselves. Datasets and benchmarks are therefore not passive inputs to model development. They are the evidentiary infrastructure that connects model claims to language communities and deployment settings.

This sets up Section 6. Once resources, benchmarks, and evaluation risks are visible, the review can formalize the foundation-model-era taxonomy that links resource conditions, language conditions, model families, adaptation methods, supervision strategies, and evaluation settings. That taxonomy is necessary because no single dataset type, benchmark score, or model family can explain low-resource ASR reliability on its own.
