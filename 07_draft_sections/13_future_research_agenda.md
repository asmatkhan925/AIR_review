# 13. Future Research Agenda

## 13.1 From Gap Analysis to Research Priorities

Section 12 identified the cross-block bottlenecks that remain after foundation models changed the starting point of low-resource ASR. Section 13 converts those bottlenecks into research priorities. The agenda is not a list of speculative technologies. It is a reliability agenda grounded in C-G8 and supported by C-G1-C-G7: future progress should be measured by reliability, reproducibility, deployability, documentation quality, adaptation fit, evaluation transparency, compute awareness, and robustness for underrepresented languages and speech communities.

The agenda aligns with RQ1-RQ6. RQ1 requires clearer definitions of low-resource conditions. RQ2 and RQ4 require model and adaptation comparisons that expose data, compute, and forgetting constraints. RQ3 requires better resource documentation and quality control. RQ5 requires more reliable pseudo-labeling and KD. RQ6 requires evaluation beyond aggregate WER/CER, including robustness, reproducibility, hallucination, multimodal, and deployment risks. The central message is unchanged: foundation models improve the initial condition, but reliability depends on the full evidence chain.

## 13.2 Standardizing What "Low-Resource" Means

The first priority is definitional. Low-resource ASR papers should report more than labeled hours. Labeled hours remain important, but they are only one part of the resource condition. Future studies should state the amount and quality of labeled speech, the availability of unlabeled speech, whether weakly labeled or web-mined data are used, how validation and test sets were created, and whether the target language has dialect, speaker, domain, channel, orthography, script, and compute constraints. Under-resourced-language surveys and challenge evidence support this multidimensional framing [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey; @peterson2022_openasr21].

This priority follows directly from GAP-G1, GAP-G2, and GAP-G3. If one paper uses "low-resource" to mean a few labeled hours, another uses it to mean broad multilingual coverage but weak validation, and a third uses it to mean domain mismatch or compute scarcity, their results are not directly comparable. Standard reporting should therefore include labeled hours, unlabeled hours where relevant, weak-label sources, validation quality, dialect and speaker coverage, domain and channel coverage, transcript conventions, normalization policy, and compute or deployment constraints. Without that information, low-resource ASR remains a loose label rather than a reproducible research condition.

## 13.3 Better Dataset Documentation, Metadata, and Community-Aware Resource Development

The second priority is dataset documentation. Future low-resource ASR work should treat documentation as part of the evidence, not as an appendix. Resource papers and benchmark reports should describe data provenance, consent and licensing, access conditions, speaker and dialect metadata where ethically appropriate, regional or accent coverage, domain and channel composition, validation protocol, transcript conventions, normalization rules, and scoring scripts. Common Voice, dataset-quality work, and AfriSpeech-200 show why open or multilingual resources still need careful interpretation through quality, metadata, and benchmark design [@ardila2020commonvoice; @lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200].

Community-aware resource development should be a long-term priority. Low-resource ASR often concerns languages for which the relevant speech communities, educators, clinicians, broadcasters, or local researchers have knowledge that cannot be inferred from a web corpus. Metadata categories should not be collected mechanically or invasively, but the absence of metadata can make dialect, domain, and speaker-group failures invisible. Future resource work should therefore combine language expertise, community consent, clear licensing, auditable data statements, and transparent validation.

Methodological documentation frameworks can support this agenda, but they should not be confused with ASR evidence. Datasheets for Datasets, Data Statements, and Model Cards provide useful reporting concepts for datasets, language resources, and model use, while energy-reporting work supports compute transparency [@gebru2021_datasheets_for_datasets; @bender2018_data_statements; @mitchell2019_model_cards; @strubell2019_energy_policy]. In this review, these sources are background-support references only. They help specify reporting expectations; they do not establish ASR performance.

## 13.4 Orthography-Aware and Dialect-Aware Benchmarks

Future benchmarks should treat orthography and dialect as first-class evaluation variables. Orthographic variation, spelling alternatives, punctuation, casing, diacritics, word spacing, morphology, script choice, and code-switching can all change the measured error rate. If scoring rules are hidden, two systems may look comparable while using different transcript conventions. Lenient and orthography-aware evaluation evidence shows why scoring should account for acceptable variants where the language and task justify it [@karita2023lenient].

Dialect-aware benchmark design is equally important. Foundation-model averages can hide whether a model performs well on well-represented varieties while failing on underrepresented dialects, domains, or speaker groups. Future benchmarks should report language-wise, dialect-wise, domain-wise, and pooled results separately where metadata permit. They should also state whether scores are raw, normalized, macro-averaged, or pooled. FLEURS, ML-SUPERB, and AfriSpeech-200 provide useful benchmark anchors, but they also show why coverage and metadata must be read carefully [@conneau2022fleurs; @shi2023mlsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200].

This priority connects GAP-G3 and GAP-G4. The field needs benchmark reports that publish normalization rules, scoring scripts, transcript examples, language-specific handling of scripts and morphology, and subgroup breakdowns. Without these controls, reported gains may reflect formatting compatibility rather than recognition improvement, and aggregate scores may obscure the communities that motivated the benchmark.

## 13.5 Compute-Efficient, Forgetting-Aware Adaptation

Future adaptation research should move from method promotion to condition-controlled comparison. Full fine-tuning, continued pretraining, adapters, LoRA, QLoRA, prompting, and transfer are all useful in some settings, but none is universally best. Studies should report the base model and checkpoint, adaptation data, trainable parameters, memory, hardware, training cost, inference cost, decoding settings, and target-domain validation. They should also compare methods under matched data and compute budgets where possible.

GAP-G5 and GAP-G6 are especially important here. PEFT methods are attractive for low-resource ASR because they may reduce adaptation cost and support modular deployment, but general PEFT papers are method anchors rather than direct ASR evidence unless evaluated in speech settings [@pmlr-v97-houlsby19a; @hu2022lora; @dettmers2023qlora]. ASR-specific adapter and new-language adaptation evidence should carry stronger claims, especially when forgetting is evaluated [@udupa24_interspeech; @qian24_interspeech].

GAP-G12 adds the deployment dimension. If a method improves accuracy but requires compute that small labs or language communities cannot reproduce, its practical low-resource value is limited. Compute-normalized reporting should therefore include training and inference cost, memory, hardware, model size, trainable-parameter count, and reproducibility artifacts. Energy and policy reporting work is useful as background for why compute must be made visible, but ASR-specific adaptation claims must still come from speech evidence [@strubell2019_energy_policy].

## 13.6 Reliable Pseudo-Labeling and Knowledge Distillation

Pseudo-labeling and KD should remain central future directions because verified labels are often scarce. The priority is not simply to generate more labels. The priority is to make generated supervision auditable. Future studies should report teacher identity, teacher training source where known, decoding settings, confidence and uncertainty measures, filtering thresholds, retained-data proportions where possible, normalization policy, label-space compatibility, and validation behavior by language, domain, or dialect.

GAP-G7 and GAP-G8 define the core supervision agenda. Teacher errors remain difficult to detect, and multi-teacher KD needs clearer disagreement analysis and reproducible selection rules. Self-training, Noisy Student, uncertainty-driven filtering, multilingual distillation, and large-scale low-resource pseudo-labeling all support the promise of supervision expansion, but they also show why teacher quality and filtering cannot be hidden [@park20d_interspeech; @khurana2020dust; @leal21_interspeech; @bhogale24_interspeech].

Future work should evaluate pseudo-label damage, not only pseudo-label gains. That means checking whether generated labels amplify dialect bias, normalize away valid variants, increase hallucinated content, or improve aggregate scores while harming a subgroup. Multi-teacher KD should report teacher disagreement, label-space mapping, orthographic compatibility, and the policy for resolving conflicts. Without these controls, pseudo-labeling and KD may appear to solve scarcity while moving errors into the training signal.

## 13.7 Evaluation Beyond Aggregate WER/CER

WER and CER should remain part of low-resource ASR evaluation, but future evaluation must make clear that they are necessary rather than sufficient. Aggregate WER/CER should be accompanied by language-wise, dialect-wise, domain-wise, channel-wise, and speaker-group reporting where ethically and practically possible. Scores should state tokenization, punctuation, casing, script handling, normalization, and pooling policy.

GAP-G4, GAP-G9, and GAP-G12 define this evaluation agenda. Robustness protocols should test noise, far-field speech, overlap, conversational speech, and channel shift when those conditions matter for deployment. Hallucination and unsupported-content checks are needed for generative ASR and LLM-assisted correction, because fluent output can be wrong in ways that ordinary WER does not expose [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper]. Contextual and named-entity evaluation should check whether a model truly recognized the audio or used leaked context.

Future papers should also publish scoring scripts, normalization rules, evaluation splits, model versions, decoding settings, and reproducibility artifacts where possible. Toolkits and benchmarks such as SUPERB, ESPnet, SpeechBrain, and Kaldi provide infrastructure and comparability support, but the presence of a toolkit is not itself proof that a particular low-resource result is reproducible [@yang2021superb; @watanabe2018espnet; @ravanelli2021speechbrain; @povey2011kaldi]. Evaluation should therefore be reported as a protocol, not only as a number.

## 13.8 Multimodal, AVSR, SpeechLM, and LLM-Assisted ASR Under Reliability Controls

Multimodal and LLM-assisted directions should be part of the future agenda, but only under reliability controls. Low-resource AVSR needs broader multilingual and multidialect resources, clear audio-visual alignment, missing-modality tests, privacy safeguards, and evaluation under degraded audio. AV-HuBERT and MuAViC support the promise of multimodal robustness, but current evidence does not justify claiming that AVSR is mature for all low-resource ASR settings [@shi2022avhubert; @anwar23_interspeech].

Speech-text and SpeechLM systems also need bounded interpretation. SeamlessM4T and OpusLM show that speech, text, translation, and language-modeling objectives are increasingly connected, but ASR, speech translation, and post-ASR correction remain different tasks [@seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt; @tian25b_interspeech]. Future research should state which task is being evaluated, whether the output is a transcript, translation, correction, or normalized text, and how source grounding is checked.

LLM-assisted correction and rescoring should be constrained by N-best lists, lattices, confidence scores, or validated context. Unconstrained rewriting risks hallucination, over-correction, semantic drift, context leakage, and named-entity hallucination. Watchlist sources on LLM correction, hallucination benchmarks, and contextual ASR are useful for defining future evaluation needs, but they should not be used as central proof that these methods work reliably across low-resource languages [@ma2024asrerrorcorrection; @koudounas2025shallow; @wang2025contextasrbench]. GAP-G10 and GAP-G11 should therefore be read as reliability requirements, not as a call to add multimodal or LLM layers everywhere.

## 13.9 A Practical Reporting Checklist for Future Low-Resource ASR Studies

Future low-resource ASR studies should provide enough information for readers to interpret, reproduce, and compare the result. A practical report should identify the language, dialects or varieties where known, speaker and domain coverage, labeled hours, unlabeled hours, weakly labeled or web-mined sources, metadata availability, transcript policy, orthographic and normalization rules, and access or licensing conditions. It should state the base model and checkpoint, adaptation method, trainable parameters, compute and hardware, memory, inference cost, and deployment constraints.

If pseudo-labeling or KD is used, the report should identify the teacher, decoding setup, confidence or uncertainty rules, filtering thresholds, retained data, teacher disagreement policy, and validation method. If LLM-assisted correction, rescoring, contextual biasing, or AVSR is used, the report should state the constraints, input modalities, context source, missing-modality behavior, source-grounding checks, hallucination and over-correction tests, and privacy implications. Evaluation should include WER/CER plus additional protocols where needed, public scoring scripts where possible, split definitions, subgroup breakdowns, robustness checks, and reproducibility artifacts.

This checklist is intentionally broad because the low-resource condition is broad. It is not a demand that every study solve every problem. It is a demand that every study make its resource condition, model choice, adaptation path, supervision source, evaluation protocol, and deployment cost visible. That visibility is the basis for cumulative progress.

## 13.10 Section Takeaway

The future of low-resource ASR should be judged by reliability, reproducibility, and deployability, not only by aggregate accuracy. Foundation models have changed the starting point, but the field still needs better definitions, better resource documentation, orthography-aware and dialect-aware benchmarks, compute-efficient adaptation, reliable pseudo-labeling and KD, subgroup and robustness evaluation, hallucination-aware protocols, multimodal resources where justified, constrained LLM assistance, and transparent deployment reporting.

The conclusion can now return to the review's central answer. Low-resource ASR in the foundation-model era is not a solved problem and not a single-method problem. It is a coordinated reliability problem across resources, language, models, adaptation, supervision, evaluation, robustness, reproducibility, compute, and community-aware development.
