# 6. Foundation-Model-Era Taxonomy of Low-Resource ASR

## 6.1 Why a New Taxonomy Is Needed

Sections 3-5 establish the need for a taxonomy that is specific to the foundation-model era. Section 3 defined low-resource ASR as a multidimensional reliability condition rather than a simple shortage of labeled hours. Section 4 showed that foundation speech models change the starting point by making transfer, weak supervision, and multilingual pretraining more powerful. Section 5 showed that resource and benchmark quality determine whether those model gains can be interpreted. The taxonomy in this section is the review's organizing contribution: it connects resource conditions, language conditions, model families, adaptation methods, supervision strategies, and evaluation settings into a single framework.

This taxonomy is not a generic ASR taxonomy. A generic taxonomy could list hybrid systems, end-to-end systems, self-supervised encoders, multilingual recognizers, and speech-language models as successive model families. That would repeat Section 4 and miss the central low-resource problem. In the foundation-model era, the crucial question is not only which model family is used, but how that model interacts with available resources, language variation, adaptation constraints, supervision reliability, and evaluation design. Low-resource ASR therefore needs a layered taxonomy that makes these dependencies visible.

The six layers also prevent overclaiming. Foundation models may provide stronger initialization, but their success still depends on data quality, language fit, adaptation choices, and benchmark interpretation [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. Similarly, multilingual benchmarks improve comparability, but they do not remove the need to report language, dialect, domain, and normalization conditions [@shi2023mlsuperb; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. The taxonomy formalizes this review's main argument: low-resource ASR reliability is produced by interactions across layers, not by model scale alone.

## 6.2 Resource Layer

The resource layer describes what evidence is available for training, adaptation, validation, and evaluation. Labeled speech supports supervised learning and test-set construction, but it can be narrow, inconsistently normalized, or weakly documented. Unlabeled speech supports self-supervised learning and continued pretraining, but it cannot by itself verify recognition quality. Weakly labeled or web-mined speech can provide scale, while also introducing source noise, segmentation errors, language-identification problems, and transcript mismatch. Pseudo-labeled resources can expand supervision, but they depend on teacher reliability, filtering, and calibration. Multilingual corpora support transfer and comparison, but their usefulness depends on coverage, balance, metadata, and domain. Multimodal or audio-visual resources can improve robustness, but they add alignment, privacy, and coverage constraints.

This layer explains why resource quantity and resource reliability must be separated. Common Voice, VoxPopuli, and MLS illustrate how public or multilingual corpora can expand the evidence base for low-resource ASR [@ardila2020commonvoice; @wang2021voxpopuli; @pratap20_interspeech]. Dataset-quality evidence shows why such resources still require careful validation, sociolinguistic awareness, and documentation [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. For the foundation-model era, the resource layer is not simply the input to the model. It is the evidence base that determines whether transfer, adaptation, and evaluation claims are trustworthy.

## 6.3 Language Layer

The language layer captures the fact that languages are not homogeneous labels. Dialect variation affects acoustic patterns, pronunciation, vocabulary, and code choice. Orthographic inconsistency affects how speech is represented in reference transcripts and how errors are counted. Code-switching changes tokenization and language modeling. Morphology can increase surface-form variation and complicate word-level scoring. Script complexity affects segmentation, diacritics, spacing, punctuation, and normalization. Language-family mismatch affects whether representations learned from high-resource or related languages transfer well.

Language conditions affect both model behavior and evaluation. A model can fail because its pretraining or adaptation data do not cover the relevant variety; an evaluation can mislead because the scoring pipeline treats plausible orthographic variants as errors. Benchmarks such as ML-SUPERB 2.0, the ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge, IndicSUPERB, and AfriSpeech-200 make parts of this problem visible by focusing on language varieties, regional coverage, and accent or domain metadata [@shi2024mlsuperb2; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge; @javed2023indsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200]. Orthography-aware evaluation evidence further shows that transcript conventions and naturally occurring spelling variation can change measured error rates [@karita2023lenient]. The language layer therefore prevents the review from treating "language coverage" as equivalent to "language reliability."

## 6.4 Model Layer

The model layer summarizes the architecture families without turning this section into another model history. Hybrid ASR systems represent the pre-foundation baseline in which acoustic models, lexicons, language models, and decoding were often engineered separately. End-to-end ASR reduced some pipeline boundaries but still depended on paired speech-text data and task-specific evaluation [@prabhavalkar2023_e2e_asr_survey]. Self-supervised speech encoders such as wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, and WavLM made unlabeled audio central to representation learning [@mohamed2022_ssl_review; @baevski2020wav2vec2; @hsu2021hubert; @chen2022wavlm]. Multilingual SSL and weakly supervised systems such as XLS-R, Whisper, and MMS changed the starting point for many low-resource settings by enabling stronger transfer and broader language coverage [@babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms].

Foundation speech models, speech-language models, and multimodal speech-text systems extend this layer beyond acoustic recognition alone. SeamlessM4T and OpusLM, for example, show how speech, text, translation, and generation objectives can be combined in broader speech-language systems [@seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt; @tian25b_interspeech]. The taxonomy treats these systems as part of the model layer, but it also marks a boundary: translation-centric or early SpeechLM evidence should not be treated as proof that low-resource ASR is solved. Model families are one layer in the taxonomy, not the taxonomy itself.

## 6.5 Adaptation Layer

The adaptation layer describes how a pretrained or multilingual model is made useful for a target language, domain, or deployment condition. Full fine-tuning updates the entire model and can be effective, but it may overfit scarce labeled data, require substantial compute, or harm existing multilingual capabilities. Continued pretraining can specialize a model using target or related unlabeled speech before supervised adaptation [@getman24_interspeech]. Adapter tuning, LoRA, and QLoRA represent parameter-efficient strategies that reduce trainable parameters or memory requirements, but their ASR evidence is uneven and should be separated from general NLP method evidence [@pmlr-v97-houlsby19a; @hu2022lora; @dettmers2023qlora].

The taxonomy also includes prompting, contextual biasing, transfer learning, and forgetting control. Prompting and contextual biasing are relevant for speech-language and LLM-assisted systems, but they require safeguards against leakage, over-correction, or semantic drift. Transfer learning is the dominant foundation-era assumption: a model learned from other languages or domains can be adapted to a new low-resource setting. Forgetting control matters when adding a new language to an existing multilingual system because improving the new language may degrade previous capabilities [@qian24_interspeech]. Direct ASR adaptation evidence, including adapter pre-training and low-resource adapter tuning, helps ground this layer in speech-specific work [@udupa24_interspeech]. Section 8 will develop these trade-offs in detail.

## 6.6 Supervision Layer

The supervision layer distinguishes how learning signals are created and trusted. Supervised learning uses human-verified paired speech and text. Self-supervised learning uses unlabeled speech to learn representations. Self-training uses model-generated labels to retrain or adapt ASR systems. Pseudo-label filtering tries to decide which generated labels are reliable enough to use. Knowledge distillation transfers information from teacher models to student models, either from a single teacher or from multiple teachers. Agreement-based teacher selection treats teacher disagreement as a signal about label quality and risk.

This layer is necessary because foundation models often blur the boundary between data and supervision. A pseudo-labeled corpus can appear to increase labeled data, but it may also amplify teacher errors. Self-training and noisy-student evidence show that pseudo-labels can improve ASR when label quality and training conditions are controlled [@kahn2019selftraining; @park20d_interspeech]. Uncertainty-driven self-training and large-scale pseudo-labeling work show why filtering and evaluator design matter [@khurana2020dust; @bhogale24_interspeech]. Multilingual student-teacher and distillation evidence further shows that KD can support multilingual or compressed ASR, but teacher choice and disagreement remain supervision-quality problems [@leal21_interspeech; @farooq2023must; @ferraz2023multilingualdistilwhisper]. Section 9 will use this layer to separate useful supervision expansion from error propagation.

## 6.7 Evaluation Layer

The evaluation layer defines what counts as evidence of progress. WER and CER remain necessary, but they are not sufficient when used as pooled scores without information about orthography, dialect, domain, robustness, fairness, reproducibility, and compute. Orthography-aware evaluation addresses normalization, script, spelling, punctuation, and formatting. Dialect-wise and domain/channel-wise evaluation expose variation hidden by aggregate scores. Noise, far-field, and overlapping-speech robustness evaluate conditions beyond clean read speech. Fairness and demographic evaluation test whether performance differs by speaker population or recording provenance. Reproducibility and compute/deployment reporting determine whether methods can be validated and used outside large labs.

This layer also includes multimodal and future-facing evaluation concerns. AVSR robustness asks whether audio-visual information improves recognition under noisy or degraded audio conditions [@shi2022avhubert; @shi2022robustavsr; @anwar23_interspeech]. Hallucination and over-correction evaluation become important as ASR systems are connected to generative or LLM-assisted components; speech-to-text hallucination evidence shows why errors beyond WER can matter [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper]. Fairness and demographic evidence show that aggregate ASR performance can hide systematic disparities [@koenecke2020racialdisparities; @liu2021casualconversations]. Reproducibility benchmarks and robustness challenges further show that evaluation should include generalization, cost, and deployment conditions rather than only accuracy [@yang2021superb; @cornell2023chime7]. Sections 10 and 11 will expand these evaluation and multimodal issues.

## 6.8 How the Layers Interact

The taxonomy should not be read as six independent boxes. Low-resource ASR failures often arise from interactions among layers. A strong foundation model may fail because its pretraining languages do not match the target language family, because the target corpus represents only one dialect, or because the test set uses a different orthographic convention. A pseudo-labeling pipeline may increase the amount of supervision while also reproducing teacher errors on noisy channels or underrepresented speakers. A multilingual benchmark may report an improved average score while hiding poor performance on a specific domain, variety, or script. A parameter-efficient adaptation method may reduce compute while producing a trade-off between new-language performance and forgetting.

The taxonomy's purpose is to make these interactions visible before the review turns to specific solution families. Resource conditions shape what data-centric strategies are needed. Language conditions determine how adaptation and evaluation should be interpreted. Model families define the available starting points. Adaptation methods decide how those starting points are specialized. Supervision strategies determine whether additional training signals are reliable. Evaluation settings decide whether the resulting claim is scientifically meaningful. In the foundation-model era, reliable low-resource ASR depends on the alignment of all six layers.

## 6.9 Section Takeaway

This section presents the review's foundation-model-era taxonomy of low-resource ASR. The taxonomy is a central contribution because it explains why model scaling alone cannot define progress for underrepresented languages. Reliable low-resource ASR depends on how resources, language conditions, model families, adaptation methods, supervision strategies, and evaluation settings interact.

The next section turns to the first of these solution layers in detail: data-centric strategies. After the taxonomy clarifies the structure of the problem, Section 7 examines why corpus creation, transcript normalization, filtering, augmentation, validation, and metadata remain central even when strong foundation models are available.
