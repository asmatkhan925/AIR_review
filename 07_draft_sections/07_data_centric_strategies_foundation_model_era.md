# 7. Data-Centric Strategies in the Foundation-Model Era

## 7.1 Why Data-Centric Work Still Matters

Section 6 framed low-resource ASR as an interaction among resource, language, model, adaptation, supervision, and evaluation layers. Section 7 develops the first practical consequence of that taxonomy: data-centric work remains central even when strong pretrained or foundation speech models are available. Foundation models improve the starting point. They can reduce the need to train recognizers from scratch, support zero-shot or few-shot use, and make unlabeled or weakly labeled speech more useful. They do not, however, make the target data reliable, representative, normalized, or evaluation-ready by themselves.

This distinction is important because model scale can amplify poor data as well as exploit good data. A large multilingual or weakly supervised model may learn useful representations from diverse sources, but it may also inherit source imbalance, transcript noise, language-identification errors, formatting conventions, and domain skew. Data-centric strategy therefore shifts the question from "how much data is available?" to "what kind of evidence does the data provide, and what risks does it introduce?" In low-resource ASR, this question covers corpus creation, validation, documentation, transcript normalization, segmentation, filtering, augmentation, metadata, and evaluation design.

The foundation-model era makes these operations more important, not less. Weakly supervised systems such as Whisper and multilingual systems such as MMS show that scale and multilingual coverage can change baseline expectations [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. At the same time, heterogeneous speech-to-text foundation-model data evidence shows that source selection, filtering, punctuation, truecasing, and label refinement remain active design choices rather than minor preprocessing steps [@tian24_interspeech]. Data quality audit work makes the same point for public multilingual resources: transcript, audio, silence, and sociolinguistic planning problems can affect reliability even in widely used datasets [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. Section 7 therefore answers RQ3 by treating data work as an evidence-control problem for foundation-model-era ASR.

## 7.2 Corpus Creation, Validation, and Documentation

Corpus creation is not only collection. It is the process of deciding which speech events, speakers, dialects, domains, channels, scripts, licenses, and transcript conventions will define the evidence base for a language or setting. In low-resource ASR, those decisions are often more consequential than model choice because the available corpus may become the training set, validation set, benchmark, and community reference point at the same time. A corpus with many utterances can still be weak evidence if it lacks validation, has narrow speaker coverage, uses inconsistent transcript conventions, or does not document access and licensing conditions.

Common Voice illustrates the value and risk of open crowdsourced collection. Its contribution is not only multilingual scale, but also an open participation and validation model that can support underrepresented languages [@ardila2020commonvoice]. The same openness makes validation, language balance, accent coverage, and transcript quality central issues. Babel and OpenASR represent a different corpus logic: constrained challenge resources for low-resource and conversational telephone speech settings, where controlled evaluation design is part of the research value [@gales2014_babel_project_research_cued; @peterson2022_openasr21]. MLS and VoxPopuli extend the resource landscape through multilingual corpus construction and unlabeled or semi-supervised use, but their domains also constrain the claims they can support [@pratap20_interspeech; @wang2021voxpopuli].

Metadata is a data-centric strategy because it determines whether later results can be interpreted. Speaker, dialect, accent, domain, channel, collection source, and validation metadata make it possible to detect coverage gaps and subgroup failures. AfriSpeech-200 is useful in this section because accent and domain metadata are part of its contribution, but it should be treated as evidence for metadata-aware corpus design rather than as a universal low-resource benchmark [@olatunji2023_afrispeech200]. Multilingual benchmark resources such as FLEURS and ML-SUPERB can help test whether a data pipeline generalizes across languages, but they still need source documentation, normalization transparency, and subgroup-aware reporting [@conneau2022fleurs; @shi2023mlsuperb]. Dataset-quality audit evidence similarly supports the need for validation and documentation, but it should not be read as a performance claim about a particular recognizer [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. Documentation frameworks can support this agenda, but methodological documentation papers should be cited as documentation support only when they are in the bibliography and should not be treated as ASR performance evidence.

The practical implication is that Section 7 treats corpus creation as a reliability pipeline. Before asking which model to fine-tune, a low-resource ASR study should document the corpus source, collection process, speaker and variety coverage, consent and licensing conditions, transcript policy, validation procedure, split construction, and intended evaluation use. Without these details, a later WER or CER result may look precise while resting on unclear evidence.

## 7.3 Transcript Normalization and Orthographic Control

Transcript normalization is part of the ASR task in low-resource settings, not a cosmetic cleanup step. Punctuation, casing, spelling variants, diacritics, spacing, numerals, abbreviations, script choices, and tokenization all affect the mapping between speech and written reference text. In high-resource settings these conventions may be standardized by large corpora and mature evaluation scripts. In low-resource settings they may be unstable, community-specific, underdocumented, or inherited from a dataset pipeline built for another language or domain.

This matters because transcript conventions affect both training and scoring. A model trained on inconsistent transcripts learns a noisier target distribution. A model evaluated against inconsistently normalized references may be penalized for plausible variants rather than recognition failures. Orthographic inconsistency can therefore make reported gains look like modeling improvements when they are partly formatting alignment, or make a useful system look worse because the scorer treats acceptable written variants as errors. The consequence is not that one universal normalization rule should be imposed across languages. The consequence is that normalization and scoring rules must be documented and, where appropriate, evaluated in both raw and normalized forms.

The local evidence supports this point from multiple directions. Dataset-quality audit work links transcript quality to broader multilingual speech reliability [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. Heterogeneous foundation-model data-pipeline evidence shows that punctuation and truecasing decisions remain relevant even in large speech-to-text training mixtures [@tian24_interspeech]. Punctuation-restoration evidence is useful here as a transcript-formatting anchor, but it should be used carefully because it is not direct evidence that punctuation modeling improves low-resource ASR recognition [@chen21d_interspeech]. Orthography-aware evaluation evidence shows why spelling variation can change measured error rates and why language-aware scoring should be part of the evaluation protocol [@karita2023lenient]. Section 10 will return to metric design; Section 7 establishes that metric risk begins with transcript preparation.

## 7.4 Filtering, Segmentation, and Heterogeneous Data Quality

Foundation-model-era ASR depends heavily on data mixtures. Public speech corpora, parliamentary recordings, audiobooks, challenge data, web audio, subtitles, automatically segmented speech, and weak transcripts can all enter a training or adaptation pipeline. These sources are not equivalent. They differ in noise, overlap, speaker style, segmentation, language identity, transcript timing, punctuation, casing, and domain. A model trained on a large heterogeneous mixture may become more robust, but it may also learn systematic errors from the easiest, largest, or most poorly filtered sources.

Filtering and segmentation are therefore data-centric interventions. Segmentation decides what counts as a training example. Language identification decides whether an utterance belongs to the target language or to a related, mixed, or mislabeled source. Alignment decides whether the transcript corresponds to the audio. Filtering decides which samples are trustworthy enough to keep, which should be downweighted, and which should be excluded. In low-resource ASR, these decisions can change the effective training distribution more than the nominal number of hours.

VoxPopuli is a useful anchor for the role of unlabeled and transcribed multilingual speech resources in representation learning and semi-supervised settings [@wang2021voxpopuli]. Heterogeneous speech-to-text foundation-model data-pipeline evidence gives a more direct foundation-era example: source heterogeneity, proxy-task filtering, punctuation, and truecasing are all part of the training-data problem [@tian24_interspeech]. GigaSpeech-style corpus-pipeline evidence is marked locally as secondary or pending, so it can support the general point that forced alignment, segmentation, and filtering are active corpus-construction operations, but it should not carry a central claim by itself. The stronger manuscript claim should rest on verified corpus and pipeline anchors rather than on watchlist-only data-pipeline papers.

The benefit of filtering is interpretability and noise reduction. The risk is selection bias. Aggressive filtering may remove difficult accents, noisy channels, code-switched utterances, minority dialects, or atypical speakers exactly because they are hard. Data-centric strategy must therefore balance cleanliness against representativeness. For low-resource ASR, the best pipeline is not simply the one that produces the cleanest training set. It is the one whose filtering choices are documented and whose evaluation checks whether important varieties were excluded.

## 7.5 Augmentation and Robustness-Oriented Data Diversification

Augmentation is one of the clearest examples of a data-centric strategy that remains useful after foundation models. Speed perturbation and related audio augmentation can increase acoustic variation without requiring new recordings [@ko15_interspeech]. SpecAugment shows that feature-space masking can improve robustness by preventing a model from overfitting to narrow time-frequency patterns [@park19e_interspeech]. These methods remain relevant because low-resource corpora are often narrow in speaker, channel, noise, and recording condition. Augmentation can expose a model to controlled variation and reduce dependence on a small set of observed conditions.

The benefit is not unlimited. Augmentation is strongest when it simulates plausible variation that the system should handle: speaking-rate differences, channel changes, background noise, reverberation, or partial masking. It is weaker when it is used as a substitute for missing linguistic, dialectal, demographic, or domain coverage. No amount of generic noise augmentation can prove that a model handles a dialect absent from training and evaluation metadata. Similarly, speed perturbation cannot replace validation on real conversational, telephony, far-field, or accented speech if those conditions define the deployment setting.

Robustness-oriented data design should therefore combine augmentation with representative evaluation. CHiME-style robustness evidence shows why distant, multi-device, and overlapping speech conditions require explicit testing rather than assumptions from clean speech [@cornell2023chime7]. Multimodal and AVSR work extends this idea to cases where visual information may help under degraded audio, but those resources introduce their own coverage and evaluation constraints [@anwar23_interspeech]. For Section 7, the key point is bounded: augmentation is a practical data-centric tool for diversifying training conditions, but it does not replace metadata, validation, or domain-representative test sets.

## 7.6 Unlabeled, Weakly Labeled, and Pseudo-Labeled Data Pipelines

Unlabeled, weakly labeled, and pseudo-labeled data have different roles in the foundation-model era. Unlabeled speech supports self-supervised learning, continued pretraining, and representation adaptation. Weakly labeled speech supports scale through imperfect transcripts, captions, or indirect supervision. Pseudo-labeled speech uses model-generated transcripts as a form of supervision expansion. These sources can make low-resource ASR feasible when human transcription is scarce, but they also move reliability problems into the data pipeline.

Self-supervised and multilingual pretraining evidence shows why unlabeled speech matters: models can learn useful speech representations before task-specific labels are available [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @babu22_interspeech]. VoxPopuli and other multilingual resources show how unlabeled and transcribed speech can support representation learning and semi-supervised use [@wang2021voxpopuli]. Weakly supervised foundation ASR shows a different path: large weakly labeled mixtures can produce strong general ASR systems [@pmlr-v202-radford23a]. In all three cases, however, the data are not automatically reliable for a target low-resource language or domain. They require source documentation, filtering, validation, and task-specific evaluation.

Pseudo-labeling should be introduced here only as a bridge to Section 9. From a data-centric perspective, pseudo-labels are generated transcripts that must be selected, filtered, and validated. Noisy Student and uncertainty-driven self-training evidence show that pseudo-label quality, confidence, uncertainty, and filtering affect whether generated labels help or harm training [@park20d_interspeech; @khurana2020dust]. Large-scale low-resource pseudo-labeling evidence reinforces the point that pseudo-labels can expand supervision, but only under reliability controls [@bhogale24_interspeech]. Section 9 will develop teacher quality, teacher disagreement, KD, and calibration in detail. Section 7's narrower claim is that pseudo-labeled data should be treated as noisy data requiring evidence controls, not as free human supervision.

## 7.7 Data-Centric Strategy Decision Matrix

A data-centric strategy should be chosen from the failure mode, not from a generic recipe. If labeled speech is scarce but unlabeled target-language speech exists, the priority is usually SSL reuse, continued pretraining, or semi-supervised adaptation, followed by a small verified evaluation set. If transcripts are inconsistent, normalization and scoring scripts should be stabilized before model comparison. If dialect or accent coverage is weak, additional collection and metadata-aware validation should come before broad claims about language performance. If the domain or channel differs from deployment, source-specific filtering, domain-balanced splits, and robustness evaluation should be prioritized. If compute is constrained, the data strategy should produce high-quality validation and adaptation subsets rather than assuming that a larger model or larger corpus is feasible.

The framework below should guide the later table rather than replace it. Each data problem implies an intervention, a risk, and an evaluation requirement:

- Labeled scarcity: prioritize validated small corpora, transfer-ready splits, and careful dev/test construction; risk overfitting; evaluate with transparent splits and confidence about transcript quality.
- Unlabeled availability: prioritize SSL reuse, continued pretraining, or semi-supervised pipelines; risk hidden source mismatch; evaluate against verified target-language data.
- Weak labels or web-mined data: prioritize filtering, alignment, source documentation, and label refinement; risk scaling transcript noise; evaluate by source and domain.
- Orthographic inconsistency: prioritize transcript policy, normalization scripts, and raw/normalized scoring; risk formatting-driven gains; evaluate with language-aware WER/CER reporting.
- Dialect or accent mismatch: prioritize metadata-aware collection and subgroup reporting; risk pooled averages hiding failure; evaluate by variety where ethically and practically possible.
- Domain or channel mismatch: prioritize domain-balanced sampling, channel augmentation, and robustness tests; risk clean-data overclaiming; evaluate by domain and channel.
- Compute limitations: prioritize smaller verified subsets, parameter-efficient downstream plans, and reproducible recipes; risk recommending methods that cannot be reproduced; evaluate accuracy together with cost and reporting completeness.

## 7.8 Section Takeaway

Data-centric strategies remain necessary because foundation models do not remove the need for reliable evidence. Corpus creation, validation, documentation, transcript normalization, filtering, segmentation, augmentation, and pseudo-label quality controls determine whether a pretrained model can be adapted and evaluated meaningfully for a low-resource language or domain. The central lesson is that data quantity and data reliability must be separated: more data can help, but only when its sources, labels, metadata, and evaluation role are understood.

This section answers RQ3 by showing that data work is not merely preprocessing. It is the foundation-model-era mechanism for controlling what the system learns and what the benchmark can prove. Once data quality and resource conditions are stabilized, the next question is how to adapt foundation models efficiently and safely to the target language, domain, and compute setting. Section 8 therefore turns from data-centric strategy to adaptation strategy.
