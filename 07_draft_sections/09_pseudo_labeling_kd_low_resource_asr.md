# 9. Pseudo-Labeling and Knowledge Distillation for Low-Resource ASR

## 9.1 Why Supervision Expansion Matters

Sections 7 and 8 explain why low-resource ASR in the foundation-model era remains constrained by data quality and adaptation conditions. Section 9 turns to the supervision layer. Many low-resource settings do not have enough verified paired speech and text for stable supervised adaptation, but they may have unlabeled speech, weakly labeled speech, web-mined audio, or access to a stronger teacher model. Pseudo-labeling, self-training, and knowledge distillation are therefore attractive because they can expand the usable training signal without requiring full manual transcription for every utterance.

The central qualification is that supervision expansion is not automatically supervision improvement. A generated transcript becomes training data only after a teacher model, decoding setup, filtering rule, normalization policy, and validation protocol have shaped it. If the teacher is mismatched to the target language, dialect, channel, or orthography, pseudo-labels can reproduce exactly the errors that low-resource adaptation is supposed to fix. If the student is trained on these labels without reliability controls, the system may amplify teacher bias, overfit to formatting conventions, or improve aggregate WER while worsening important subgroups.

This section answers RQ5 by treating pseudo-labeling and KD as reliability-sensitive supervision mechanisms. The main synthesis claim is that pseudo-labeling and KD can expand supervision, but their value depends on teacher quality, uncertainty and confidence filtering, teacher agreement, label normalization, language and domain match, and evaluation design. This framing connects directly to Section 7's data controls and Section 8's adaptation conditions, while preparing Section 10's evaluation argument.

## 9.2 Self-Training and Pseudo-Label Generation

Self-training uses a trained ASR model to generate labels for unlabeled speech and then trains or adapts a student model using those generated labels. In low-resource ASR, this can make otherwise unused target-language or target-domain speech available for adaptation. It can also support domain adaptation when unlabeled speech is easier to obtain than manually transcribed speech. Early and recent ASR self-training evidence shows that teacher quality, pseudo-label diversity, filtering, and training conditions affect whether self-training helps [@kahn2019selftraining]. Noisy Student ASR evidence further shows that filtering, balancing, augmentation, and student training design are part of the pseudo-labeling method rather than optional details [@park20d_interspeech].

Iterative pseudo-labeling extends this idea by regenerating labels as the student improves. The benefit is that the student can gradually make better use of unlabeled speech, especially when initial labels are imperfect but still informative. Iterative pseudo-labeling and SlimIPL-style work are useful anchors for this repeated relabeling family, but they should not be treated as proof that iteration always improves low-resource ASR [@xu2020iterativepseudolabeling; @likhomanenko2020slimipl]. Iteration can also reinforce early mistakes if the filtering rules and validation checks are weak.

Large-scale pseudo-labeling provides the strongest current low-resource bridge because it treats pseudo-label selection as a reliability problem, not just a scale problem. Recent low-resource pseudo-labeling evidence using multiple base models and evaluators shows why robust pseudo-label selection matters when generated labels become the main supervision source [@bhogale24_interspeech]. The core lesson is conservative: pseudo-labeling enables larger training sets and stronger students, but teacher errors become training data unless selection and evaluation expose them.

## 9.3 Pseudo-Label Filtering, Confidence, and Uncertainty

Filtering is the analytical center of pseudo-labeling. It determines which generated labels are trusted, which are discarded, and which may be used only with lower weight or later in training. Confidence filtering uses model scores to select labels that appear reliable. Uncertainty filtering estimates instability, for example through dropout disagreement or related measures. Evaluator-based filtering uses an additional model or metric to judge generated labels. Curriculum pseudo-labeling orders examples by estimated difficulty. Calibration-aware filtering asks whether the teacher's confidence is actually meaningful for the target data.

These mechanisms matter because high confidence is not the same as correctness. A teacher can be confidently wrong under dialect mismatch, domain mismatch, noisy channels, code-switching, or orthographic variation. A model trained mostly on clean read speech may assign high confidence to normalized or overregularized transcripts that do not match the target speech community. A multilingual teacher may be confident in a related but wrong language, script, or lexical convention. Filtering must therefore be evaluated against target-language validation data whenever such data exist.

Uncertainty-driven self-training is a key anchor because it makes pseudo-label selection a first-class adaptation mechanism under domain mismatch [@khurana2020dust]. Complementarity between pretraining and self-training reinforces the same point: self-training is more useful when it is integrated with a representation and adaptation strategy, not treated as a separate data shortcut [@xu2020selftraining]. Calibration and curriculum evidence in the repository is mostly secondary, so the section uses it as support for the general reliability concept rather than as a strong central claim. The practical requirement is clear: pseudo-labeling papers should report confidence measures, uncertainty rules, thresholds, filtering stages, retained data proportions where available, and validation behavior by language or domain.

## 9.4 Knowledge Distillation: Single-Teacher, Intermediate, and Sequence-Level Distillation

Knowledge distillation transfers information from a teacher model to a student model. The general method anchor is teacher-student distillation, where the student learns from teacher outputs rather than only from hard reference labels [@hinton2015distilling]. In low-resource ASR, KD can serve several roles: compressing a large model for deployment, adapting a student to a target language, transferring multilingual knowledge, or using a stronger foundation model as a teacher for a smaller recognizer. KD should therefore not be treated only as compression. It is also a supervision-transfer mechanism, and its reliability depends on teacher-student mismatch.

Single-teacher KD is conceptually simple: one teacher supplies posterior distributions, decoded sequences, representations, or other training targets. The risk is that the student inherits the teacher's blind spots. Intermediate KD transfers hidden representations or intermediate information rather than only final outputs, and CTC/intermediate KD evidence supports keeping this family distinct from posterior-level or sequence-level transfer [@yoon2022interkd]. Sequence-level KD uses teacher-generated output sequences and is especially relevant when the teacher's decoded transcript becomes the student's target. Its risk overlaps with pseudo-labeling: the sequence may be fluent, normalized, or high-confidence while still being wrong for the target speech.

Foundation-model distillation brings these issues into sharper focus. Whisper-style or other large foundation ASR teachers can provide strong labels or compression targets, but they may also carry language coverage limits, formatting conventions, hallucination risks, and domain mismatch. Multilingual DistilWhisper evidence is useful as a foundation-ASR distillation anchor for underrepresented-language settings, but its local verification status requires cautious wording [@ferraz2023multilingualdistilwhisper]. The safe claim is that foundation-model distillation is promising for low-resource ASR, especially when deployment constraints require smaller students, but it requires teacher-error analysis and target-language validation.

## 9.5 Multi-Teacher KD and Agreement-Based Reliability

Multi-teacher KD uses more than one teacher model to provide supervision. This can reduce reliance on a single teacher's errors and may improve coverage across languages, domains, or model families. It is especially relevant in low-resource ASR because one teacher may be stronger for a related language, another for the target domain, and another for decoding or language modeling. Multi-teacher systems can also make disagreement visible: if several plausible teachers disagree on an utterance, that disagreement may signal label risk.

The difficulty is that multiple teachers often have mismatched label spaces, tokenization schemes, orthographic conventions, decoding styles, and language coverage. Self-adaptive multilingual distillation shows how teacher and student confidence can be used in multilingual ASR distillation [@leal21_interspeech]. Multilingual student-teacher learning evidence further shows that posterior mapping can be needed when teacher and student character sets differ [@farooq2023must]. These are not implementation footnotes. They show why teacher agreement, posterior mapping, label-space alignment, and selection rules are part of the reliability argument.

Agreement-based teacher selection is therefore a promising but under-standardized direction. Agreement can help identify robust labels, but it can also hide shared bias if all teachers were trained on similar data or normalize transcripts in the same way. Disagreement can identify risky utterances, but it does not automatically reveal which teacher is correct. Multi-teacher KD should therefore report teacher identities, language coverage, decoding settings, mapping rules, agreement metrics, and the policy for resolving disagreement. Without these details, multi-teacher supervision can look stronger while making error propagation harder to audit.

## 9.6 Foundation-Model-Era Pseudo-Labeling

Foundation models change pseudo-labeling by improving the teacher side of the pipeline. Whisper-like and MMS-like systems can provide stronger multilingual baselines, broader language coverage, and practical zero-shot or weakly supervised starting points [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. This can make pseudo-labeling more feasible for languages or domains where earlier teachers were too weak. A stronger teacher can generate more usable labels, support larger adaptation sets, and reduce the cost of initial transcription.

The risk is that foundation-model pseudo-labels may look more reliable than they are. A foundation model can impose its own formatting conventions, normalize away meaningful variants, hallucinate content, or perform unevenly across languages, dialects, and channels. This is especially important when a generative recognizer or speech-language model produces fluent text. Fluency can hide acoustic mismatch. Hallucination-risk evidence shows why speech-to-text systems need evaluation beyond surface WER when generated output may include unsupported content [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper].

LLM- or SpeechLLM-assisted pseudo-label refinement should therefore remain bounded in this review. The repository contains watchlist evidence for LLM-enhanced pseudo-label refinement and audio-LLM iterative refinement, but these sources should not carry central claims. They are better treated as emerging directions that require constrained correction, source-grounding, hallucination checks, and semantic-preservation evaluation. Section 11 will revisit those risks. Section 9's narrower claim is that foundation models can improve the teacher pool, but they do not remove the need for filtering, normalization, and validation.

## 9.7 Reliability Risks: Error Amplification, Orthography, Bias, and Evaluation

The main risk of pseudo-labeling and KD is error amplification. When a teacher misrecognizes a word, normalizes a dialect form incorrectly, inserts unsupported text, or chooses the wrong script convention, that output can become the student's training target. In a high-resource setting, validation sets and manual inspection may catch these problems. In a low-resource setting, the same language may lack enough verified development data to detect teacher failure reliably. This makes pseudo-labeling attractive and dangerous at the same time.

Dialect and domain mismatch intensify the problem. A teacher trained on broad multilingual or high-resource data may fail on a minority variety, noisy channel, clinical domain, conversational style, or code-switched speech. Confidence filtering can reduce noise, but high confidence can still be wrong when the teacher's uncertainty estimate is poorly calibrated for the target setting. Orthographic normalization adds another risk: pseudo-labels may look cleaner after punctuation, casing, spelling, or script normalization, while the normalization changes what is being learned and scored. This links pseudo-labeling directly to the transcript-control issues in Section 7 and the evaluation issues in Section 10.

Evaluation must therefore be part of the pseudo-labeling method. Pooled WER or CER can hide whether pseudo-labels helped one subgroup while hurting another, or whether gains came from formatting alignment rather than recognition. Pseudo-labeling and KD studies should report the teacher model, teacher training source if known, decoding settings, confidence or uncertainty rules, thresholds, filtering stages, amount of retained data where available, normalization policy, student architecture, and validation protocol. For multi-teacher systems, they should also report teacher agreement and disagreement handling. Without these details, supervision expansion cannot be distinguished from error propagation.

## 9.8 Pseudo-Labeling and KD Decision Matrix

The following decision framework summarizes when pseudo-labeling, KD, multi-teacher KD, or no pseudo-labeling yet may be appropriate:

- No labeled data and no validation set: avoid strong pseudo-labeling claims; first create a minimal verified evaluation set or use pseudo-labels only for exploratory bootstrapping.
- Small labeled dev/test set available: use it to calibrate teacher confidence, tune filtering thresholds, and evaluate pseudo-label damage by language, domain, and normalization condition.
- Abundant unlabeled target-language speech: consider self-training or iterative pseudo-labeling, but report filtering rules, retained data, and target-domain validation.
- Weakly labeled or web-mined speech: prioritize alignment, segmentation, language identification, and transcript cleaning before treating the data as supervision.
- Strong foundation-model teacher available: use it as a candidate teacher, not as a guarantee; check hallucination, formatting bias, and target-language mismatch.
- Multiple possible teachers available: consider multi-teacher KD or agreement-based selection, but report label-space mapping, teacher disagreement, and selection policy.
- Orthographic or script inconsistency: stabilize normalization and scoring before training on pseudo-labels, or report raw and normalized evaluations.
- Target domain or channel mismatch: prefer uncertainty filtering, target-domain validation, and domain-wise reporting because high-confidence labels may still be wrong.
- Strict compute or deployment constraints requiring distillation: use KD to train a smaller student, but evaluate both accuracy and deployment cost rather than compression alone.
- Contextual vocabulary or generative correction risk: use constrained correction or rescoring only with source-grounded evaluation, hallucination checks, and over-correction analysis.

## 9.9 Section Takeaway

Pseudo-labeling and KD are central low-resource ASR strategies because they can expand supervision when verified labels are scarce. Their value, however, depends on reliability controls. Teacher quality, uncertainty, confidence, agreement, normalization, language and domain match, and evaluation design determine whether generated supervision helps or amplifies errors. The most important distinction is therefore not between labeled and pseudo-labeled data, but between verified supervision and noisy supervision with transparent controls.

This conclusion leads directly to Section 10. Pseudo-labeling and KD are useful only if evaluation can detect reliability failures. The next section therefore turns to evaluation practice, robustness, reproducibility, and benchmark design: the mechanisms that determine whether low-resource ASR progress is real, comparable, and safe to interpret.
