# 4. From Hybrid ASR to Foundation Speech Models

## 4.1 Hybrid and End-to-End ASR as the Pre-Foundation Baseline

Before the foundation-model era, low-resource ASR was difficult not only because labeled speech was scarce, but because the surrounding expertise and resource infrastructure were also scarce. Hybrid ASR systems separated acoustic modeling, pronunciation lexicons, language modeling, decoding, and adaptation into distinct components. This modularity made the engineering problem interpretable, but it also meant that a low-resource language often needed expert-built lexicons, pronunciation rules, transcribed speech, text corpora, and task-specific alignments before competitive recognition could be attempted. Low-resource programs such as the Babel line of work made this dependence visible: the task was not merely to fit a model, but to build enough acoustic, lexical, and evaluation infrastructure for constrained recognition and keyword spotting [@gales2014_babel_project_research_cued]. Earlier reviews similarly framed under-resourced ASR as a problem of incomplete language, speech, and evaluation resources rather than as a single missing dataset [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey].

End-to-end ASR changed the architecture but did not remove the resource problem. By learning a more direct mapping from acoustic sequences to output tokens, E2E systems reduced some hand-engineered pipeline dependencies and made neural sequence modeling central to ASR. However, E2E models still needed enough paired speech and text to learn target-language acoustics, grapheme or subword behavior, decoding conventions, and domain regularities. The modern E2E survey literature is useful here because it shows that E2E ASR consolidated many modeling choices, but still left data, adaptation, and evaluation as central design variables [@prabhavalkar2023_e2e_asr_survey]. For low-resource ASR, the pre-foundation baseline is therefore best understood as a setting where model training, lexicon design, text normalization, and evaluation were all constrained at once.

This baseline matters for the rest of the review. Foundation models did not emerge into a blank field; they changed which parts of this infrastructure could be reused across languages and tasks. The key shift was from building nearly everything for each target language toward adapting representations, decoders, and multilingual priors learned elsewhere. That shift is substantial, but it also creates the central Section 4 question: which bottlenecks moved, and which remained?

## 4.2 Self-Supervised Speech Representation Learning

Self-supervised speech representation learning was the first major step in moving low-resource ASR away from dependence on large labeled corpora. SSL models use unlabeled speech to learn representations that can later be fine-tuned or evaluated on downstream tasks. Reviews of the SSL speech literature describe this as a broad shift in how speech systems exploit data: unlabeled audio becomes a training resource rather than unused background material [@mohamed2022_ssl_review]. wav2vec 2.0 is the clearest early anchor for this change because it showed that contrastive self-supervised pretraining could produce strong representations that improve ASR with limited labeled data [@baevski2020wav2vec2].

The next generation of SSL encoders broadened the representation-learning toolkit. HuBERT used masked prediction over hidden units, WavLM extended SSL toward broader speech processing and robustness objectives, and related models such as w2v-BERT, random-projection quantizers, and data2vec explored alternative pretraining signals [@hsu2021hubert; @chen2022wavlm; @chung2021_w2vbert; @pmlr-v162-chiu22a; @pmlr-v162-baevski22a]. For low-resource ASR, the analytical consequence is more important than the individual objective: SSL made it possible to start from reusable acoustic representations rather than from randomly initialized acoustic models.

Yet SSL did not make low-resource ASR automatic. Original SSL evidence was often strongest for well-studied languages, clean public corpora, or benchmark conditions that did not capture the full range of underrepresented-language variation. A pretrained encoder still needs a downstream ASR head, target-language or related-language adaptation, decoding choices, transcript normalization, and evaluation data. SSL helps with one part of the resource condition, namely unlabeled speech, but it does not guarantee that dialect, orthography, domain, and benchmark coverage are adequate. It should therefore be treated as a changed starting point rather than as a solved low-resource pipeline.

## 4.3 Multilingual and Weakly Supervised ASR

Multilingual SSL extended the same logic across languages. XLSR-53 and XLS-R showed that cross-lingual pretraining could make representations more transferable, especially when target-language labeled data were limited [@conneau21_interspeech; @babu22_interspeech]. This was a major change for low-resource ASR because the initial model no longer had to be learned primarily from the target language. Instead, the target language could benefit from multilingual pretraining, related-language structure, and shared acoustic or phonetic patterns. Benchmarks such as ML-SUPERB then made multilingual ASR and language-identification evaluation more systematic across large language sets [@shi2023mlsuperb].

The same period also saw weakly supervised systems reset practical expectations for baseline ASR. Whisper demonstrated that large-scale weak supervision could produce a strong multilingual ASR system with practical zero-shot and transfer value [@pmlr-v202-radford23a]. MMS pushed multilingual coverage further by scaling speech technology across a much larger language set [@pratap2024mms]. These systems are central to RQ2 because they changed what a low-resource project can reasonably start from: a new language may now begin with a pretrained multilingual or weakly supervised recognizer rather than with a small supervised model trained from scratch.

However, multilingual scale should not be confused with uniform reliability. Language inclusion does not ensure balanced data, comparable transcript conventions, dialect coverage, or deployment-domain coverage. ML-SUPERB 2.0 and the ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge are important because they move evaluation toward modeling constraints, language varieties, and inclusive ASR benchmarking rather than relying only on broad language counts [@shi2024mlsuperb2; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. The lesson for the review is that multilingual and weakly supervised ASR improve initialization and baseline creation, but they also make evaluation more demanding: average performance can hide language-level, variety-level, and domain-level weaknesses.

## 4.4 Speech Foundation Models and Speech-Language Models

The foundation-model framing broadens ASR beyond acoustic encoders and multilingual recognizers. Speech foundation models increasingly combine speech pretraining, text supervision, multitask learning, instruction-style use, and multimodal speech-text objectives. SpeechT5 and SpeechUT are useful transitional examples because they connect speech representation learning with encoder-decoder and speech-text pretraining designs [@ao2022speecht5; @zhang2022speechut]. SpeechLM-style work extends this direction by integrating speech and unpaired text more explicitly [@zhang2024_speechlm].

Speech-text and multimodal systems further expand the model space. SeamlessM4T is a strong anchor for this shift because it frames speech and text tasks jointly across many languages, including ASR-related capability, speech translation, and multilingual speech-text modeling [@seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt]. OpusLM provides an open SpeechLM example with staged training and speech-language modeling evidence [@tian25b_interspeech]. These sources matter for Section 4 because they show that the field is moving from ASR-only recognizers toward systems that embed ASR inside broader speech-language and speech-text architectures.

For this review, the boundary condition is important. Speech-language and speech-text systems should not be treated as automatic evidence that low-resource ASR is solved. Translation-centric systems must be used carefully when making ASR claims, and early SpeechLM evidence should be linked to ASR-specific evaluation where possible. Direct low-resource evaluation remains essential. The Kanyen'keha foundation-model evaluation is useful because it shows both sides of the Section 4 argument: speech foundation models can materially improve the low-resource starting point, but performance still depends on adaptation choices, language resources, and evaluation conditions [@geng25c_interspeech]. LLM-assisted correction and rescoring belong mainly to Section 11; here they are best treated as a future-facing extension of the foundation-model trajectory, not as the main account of ASR model evolution.

## 4.5 What Foundation Models Change and What They Do Not

The analytical core of this section is that foundation speech models improve the starting point of low-resource ASR, but they shift the bottleneck toward adaptation, data quality, and evaluation rather than eliminating it. The change is real. SSL models make unlabeled speech useful. Multilingual SSL improves transfer and initialization. Weakly supervised systems create stronger zero-shot and few-shot baselines. Massively multilingual systems increase the number of languages for which some pretrained capability exists. Speech-language models and speech-text systems expand the range of architectures that can support ASR-like tasks.

What does not change is equally important. A low-resource language still needs target-language evidence to expose recognition errors, transcript-normalization choices, dialect and domain mismatch, and deployment constraints. A pretrained model still needs adaptation strategies that fit the target data, compute budget, and forgetting risk. A benchmark score still needs interpretation through language coverage, source quality, and evaluation protocol. The Kanyen'keha evaluation and adaptation evidence show why direct low-resource testing matters even when strong foundation models are available [@geng25c_interspeech]. Adaptation matrices also show that foundation-model use quickly leads into Section 8 questions about full fine-tuning, continued pretraining, adapters, parameter-efficient tuning, and forgetting control rather than ending the methodological problem.

The Section 4 synthesis is therefore not that the field moved from weak methods to strong methods in a simple linear progression. It is that the unit of work changed. Earlier low-resource ASR often required building models and resources nearly from scratch. Foundation-model-era ASR often begins with a strong pretrained system, but the research challenge moves to selecting the right starting point, validating data quality, adapting under realistic constraints, and evaluating failure modes. This interpretation preserves the review's central argument: foundation models reshape low-resource ASR, but reliable recognition still depends on resource quality, language fit, adaptation design, reproducibility, compute, and robustness.

## 4.6 Section Takeaway

This section answers RQ2 by showing how ASR moved from hybrid and end-to-end systems toward SSL, multilingual, weakly supervised, and foundation speech models. The progression changed the starting point for low-resource ASR: reusable pretrained representations, multilingual transfer, weak supervision, and speech-language modeling now provide stronger baselines than earlier language-specific pipelines. But the low-resource problem did not disappear. It became more dependent on which resources and benchmarks can prove that a model works for the intended language, variety, domain, and deployment setting.

This sets up Section 5. Once model starting points improve, the quality, representativeness, documentation, and comparability of resources and benchmarks become even more important. The next section therefore turns from model families to the datasets and benchmarks that determine what low-resource ASR progress can actually mean.
