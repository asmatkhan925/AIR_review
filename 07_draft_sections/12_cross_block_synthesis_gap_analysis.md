# 12. Cross-Block Synthesis and Gap Analysis

## 12.1 Purpose of the Cross-Block Synthesis

Sections 3-11 examined the main components of low-resource ASR separately: the definition of low-resource conditions, the move from hybrid and end-to-end systems to foundation speech models, the role of resources and benchmarks, the foundation-model-era taxonomy, data-centric strategies, adaptation, pseudo-labeling and knowledge distillation, evaluation, and multimodal or LLM-assisted directions. Section 12 integrates those components. Its purpose is not to introduce another method family, but to show how the evidence fits together.

The synthesis uses the six taxonomy layers introduced earlier: resource, language, model, adaptation, supervision, and evaluation. It also uses the Core 60 reference backbone, the Block G claims C-G1-C-G8, and the Block G gaps GAP-G1-GAP-G12. The organizing question is the main review question: how should low-resource ASR be understood in the foundation-model era, and what conditions still determine reliable progress?

The answer is that low-resource ASR is a cross-layer reliability problem. Foundation models change the starting point, but they do not remove the need for data quality, language fit, constrained adaptation, reliable supervision, robust evaluation, reproducibility, compute reporting, and careful treatment of multimodal or LLM-assisted systems. This section therefore converts the review's separate evidence blocks into a single analytical argument.

## 12.2 The Central Synthesis: Foundation Models Change the Starting Point, Not the Full Reliability Problem

Before foundation models, many low-resource ASR projects began with limited labeled speech, language-specific resources, custom lexicons or pipelines, and weak baselines. Under-resourced-language surveys and challenge programs show that data scarcity, linguistic coverage, pronunciation resources, transcription quality, and evaluation design were already coupled problems rather than isolated obstacles [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey; @gales2014_babel_project_research_cued]. The older framing remains useful because it explains why low-resource ASR cannot be reduced to a model architecture problem.

Foundation speech models changed that starting point. Self-supervised and multilingual systems made it possible to begin from stronger acoustic representations. Weakly supervised and massively multilingual models made broad multilingual baselines more accessible. Speech-text and SpeechLM-style systems widened the model space beyond conventional audio-to-text recognition [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms; @seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt; @tian25b_interspeech]. In practical terms, many low-resource studies no longer start from no model; they start from a pretrained model whose coverage, training data, decoding behavior, and evaluation protocol must be understood.

This is the central synthesis claim represented by C-G2: foundation models improve the initial condition but shift the bottleneck rather than eliminate it. C-G1 explains why low-resource status remains multidimensional. C-G3 shows that data quality, filtering, normalization, metadata, and validation still shape downstream behavior. C-G4 shows that adaptation effectiveness depends on data, domain, language relatedness, compute, and forgetting. C-G5 shows that pseudo-labeling and KD can expand supervision, but only when teacher quality and filtering are controlled. C-G6 shows that evaluation based only on pooled WER or CER can hide important failures. C-G7 adds that multimodal, AVSR, SpeechLM, and LLM-assisted systems create new reliability risks. C-G8 turns these findings into a reproducible future agenda.

The review's main answer is therefore not that foundation models fail. It is that foundation models move the field from a "can we train anything?" problem toward a "can we trust, adapt, evaluate, reproduce, and deploy this system under a specific low-resource condition?" problem. The shift is substantial, but it does not make data, language, supervision, or evaluation secondary.

## 12.3 Cross-Layer Interactions: Why Bottlenecks Persist

The six taxonomy layers should not be read as independent boxes. They are interacting constraints. The same paper can sit at the resource layer because it introduces data, at the model layer because it enables transfer, and at the evaluation layer because its benchmark protocol determines what claims can be made. This is why method rankings alone are weak evidence for low-resource ASR.

The resource and language layers interact first. More hours do not automatically solve dialect mismatch, orthographic inconsistency, morphology, code-switching, or script complexity. A large corpus can still be narrow in speakers, domains, or transcription conventions. Conversely, a smaller corpus can be valuable if it has verified labels, dialect metadata, clear normalization rules, and a careful evaluation split. Multilingual resources such as Common Voice and FLEURS improve coverage, but their value still depends on documentation, balance, and evaluation design [@ardila2020commonvoice; @conneau2022fleurs].

The resource and model layers interact through scale and noise. Weakly labeled or web-mined data can train strong foundation systems, but weak labels can also encode transcript noise, domain skew, language imbalance, and formatting conventions. Whisper and MMS show the importance of large-scale weakly supervised or massively multilingual training, but their evidence should be interpreted through language coverage, data provenance, and benchmark comparability rather than as proof that scale alone solves low-resource ASR [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms].

The model and adaptation layers interact because a stronger pretrained model still needs target-language and target-domain alignment. Full fine-tuning, continued pretraining, adapters, LoRA-style methods, prompting, and forgetting-aware adaptation solve different problems. No single adaptation strategy is universally best. The proper comparison depends on labeled data, unlabeled target speech, language relatedness, domain mismatch, compute, trainable parameters, and whether the model must preserve previous multilingual capability [@udupa24_interspeech; @qian24_interspeech].

The adaptation and supervision layers interact when pseudo-labeling and KD become part of adaptation. A foundation model may be a stronger teacher, but pseudo-labels are still machine-generated supervision. Teacher errors, confidence calibration, filtering thresholds, normalization choices, and teacher disagreement determine whether self-training or KD expands useful supervision or amplifies bias. Large-scale low-resource pseudo-labeling evidence supports the promise of this route, but the value lies in selection and validation, not in treating generated transcripts as clean labels [@park20d_interspeech; @khurana2020dust; @leal21_interspeech; @bhogale24_interspeech].

The supervision and evaluation layers interact because pseudo-label gains are meaningful only if evaluation can detect teacher-induced errors. A student may improve an aggregate score while inheriting a teacher's dialect bias, normalization convention, or domain mismatch. This is why evaluation must include language, dialect, domain, orthographic, and robustness breakdowns where relevant. Benchmark and fairness evidence shows that pooled scores can hide subgroup differences, while hallucination evidence shows that fluent output may still be unsupported by the audio [@shi2023mlsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200; @koenecke2020racialdisparities; @koenecke2024carelesswhisper].

Finally, the multimodal/LLM layer interacts with evaluation. AVSR and multimodal systems can improve robustness when visual data are available and aligned, but low-resource AVSR evidence remains narrower than audio-only ASR evidence. LLM-assisted correction and contextualization can help with terminology or formatting, but unconstrained rewriting risks hallucination, over-correction, semantic drift, and context leakage. These directions should therefore be treated as reliability-sensitive extensions of the system, not as replacements for evaluation [@shi2022avhubert; @anwar23_interspeech; @ma2024asrerrorcorrection].

## 12.4 Comparative Synthesis of Solution Families

The solution families reviewed in Sections 7-11 are complementary rather than competing in a single ranking. Data-centric strategies are best suited to improving corpus quality, metadata, filtering, normalization, and validation. They work when the data sources can be audited and when transcript conventions are documented. Their residual risk is that cleaning can hide dialectal or orthographic variation if the target convention is not explicit. The evaluation requirement is to report data provenance, filtering, validation, and normalization.

Full fine-tuning and multilingual transfer are useful when a pretrained model has relevant representations and some reliable target-language or target-domain labels exist. They work best when evaluation can detect overfitting, domain mismatch, and language-family transfer limits. Continued pretraining is useful when unlabeled target speech is available and the target distribution differs from the original pretraining mixture. Its condition is data relevance and sufficient compute; its risk is over-specialization or hidden data noise.

Parameter-efficient adaptation is useful when memory, trainable-parameter count, modularity, or forgetting constraints matter. Its condition is that speech-specific evidence and matched baselines are available. General adapters, LoRA, and QLoRA are strong method anchors, but they are not all direct ASR evidence, so the residual risk is overtransferring conclusions from NLP to speech [@pmlr-v97-houlsby19a; @hu2022lora; @dettmers2023qlora]. The evaluation requirement is to report trainable parameters, memory, inference cost, target-language performance, and forgetting.

Pseudo-labeling and self-training are useful when unlabeled or weakly labeled target speech is available. Their condition is a teacher whose errors can be estimated and filtered. Their residual risk is error amplification. Single-teacher and multi-teacher KD are useful for compression, transfer, and supervision expansion, but they require teacher-student compatibility, label-space alignment, and disagreement handling. Their evaluation requirement is to report teacher identity, confidence or uncertainty rules, filtering thresholds, retained data where possible, and performance by language or domain.

Robustness-oriented evaluation is not a method family in the same sense, but it is the mechanism that makes all method claims interpretable. It is useful whenever data, model, adaptation, or supervision choices might behave differently across languages, dialects, domains, speakers, channels, or orthographic conventions. Its condition is that benchmarks and metadata allow meaningful breakdowns. Its residual risk is false comparability when pooled scores are reported without protocol details.

AVSR and multimodal systems are useful when degraded audio is a major deployment condition and reliable visual streams are available. Their residual risks are privacy, visual coverage, modality dropout, alignment, and narrow multilingual evidence. LLM-assisted correction and rescoring are useful only when they are constrained by acoustic hypotheses, confidence scores, lattices, or validated context. Their residual risks are hallucination, over-correction, context leakage, and task-boundary confusion. In both cases, the evaluation requirement is source-grounded testing rather than surface fluency.

## 12.5 Evidence Strength and Overclaiming Boundaries

The review separates evidence strength explicitly because low-resource ASR claims can be distorted by mixed source types. Established central claims should be carried by Core 60 and verified matrix evidence, especially verified-primary sources such as major benchmark, corpus, foundation-model, adaptation, pseudo-labeling, and evaluation anchors. Verified-secondary sources can support synthesis when their limits are marked, especially for sources whose primary status is pending or whose venue is less settled. Method anchors are useful for explaining mechanisms, but they should not be treated as direct ASR evidence unless they were evaluated in speech conditions.

Watchlist and arXiv-only emerging evidence is handled differently. These sources can identify frontier directions, risks, or open evaluation needs. They should not carry strong central claims about what reliably works for low-resource ASR. This rule is especially important for LLM-assisted ASR, contextual ASR, SpeechLM frontiers, and hallucination benchmarks, where the field is moving quickly and where task definitions may mix ASR, correction, speech understanding, and generation.

The same boundary applies to thesis or Pashto-related material. Such material can inform examples about transcript normalization, dialect imbalance, pseudo-label reliability, noisy channels, or multimodal robustness, but it is not the central evidence base for this field-level review. The paper is not a Pashto-centered thesis summary. It is a review of low-resource ASR in the foundation-model era, with Pashto allowed only as an illustrative example when it clarifies a broader problem.

These boundaries protect the central argument. The review does not claim that foundation models solve low-resource ASR. It does not claim that one adaptation method is universally best. It does not treat pseudo-labels as clean labels. It does not treat AVSR or LLM correction as mature universal solutions. Instead, it uses evidence strength to decide what each source can support.

## 12.6 Gap Analysis Across Blocks

The twelve Block G gaps can be synthesized into four higher-level clusters. The first cluster is definition and resource documentation. GAP-G1 shows that low-resource definitions remain inconsistent. GAP-G2 shows that dataset size is often reported without enough quality, validation, metadata, licensing, or dialect information. GAP-G3 shows that orthographic normalization and transcript comparability remain underreported. Together, these gaps explain why "more data" is an incomplete research agenda. Future work must report not only hours, but also validation status, source quality, speaker and dialect metadata where appropriate, licensing, access conditions, normalization rules, and scoring scripts.

The second cluster is model and adaptation comparability. GAP-G4 shows that aggregate foundation-model results can hide language, dialect, and domain failures. GAP-G5 shows that adaptation comparisons are not standardized across data volume, compute, and forgetting constraints. GAP-G6 shows that parameter-efficient methods are promising but direct ASR evidence remains uneven relative to general method evidence. The common problem is comparability: without matched data conditions, compute budgets, trainable-parameter reporting, and subgroup evaluation, the field cannot tell whether a gain comes from the method, the model, the data, or the evaluation setup.

The third cluster is supervision reliability. GAP-G7 shows that pseudo-label teacher errors remain difficult to detect. GAP-G8 shows that multi-teacher KD needs clearer disagreement analysis and reproducible selection rules. These gaps matter because generated supervision can scale training while also scaling mistakes. The next stage of pseudo-labeling and KD research should treat teacher confidence, uncertainty, disagreement, filtering, and language/domain mismatch as evidence variables, not implementation details.

The fourth cluster is evaluation and robustness. GAP-G9 shows that WER and CER alone are insufficient for hallucination, over-correction, and contextual ASR evaluation. GAP-G10 shows that low-resource AVSR lacks broad multilingual and multidialect resources. GAP-G11 shows that LLM-assisted ASR needs constrained correction and rescoring protocols to avoid free rewriting. GAP-G12 shows that reproducibility and compute reporting remain weak, especially for foundation-scale systems. Together, these gaps show why the future agenda must combine accuracy with robustness, source grounding, modality reliability, reproducibility, and deployment cost.

## 12.7 What the Review Adds Beyond Existing Surveys

The review's contribution is not that it lists another set of ASR models. It is not a catalogue of datasets, and it is not a thesis-specific summary. It also does not argue that foundation models solve low-resource ASR. Its contribution is to reinterpret low-resource ASR around cross-layer reliability in the foundation-model era.

First, the review contributes a foundation-model-era taxonomy that links resource, language, model, adaptation, supervision, and evaluation layers. Second, it provides a cross-block synthesis of data-centric and model-centric strategies, showing why corpus quality and model scale must be interpreted together. Third, it critically integrates adaptation, pseudo-labeling, KD, and evaluation rather than treating them as separate method families. Fourth, it builds a future agenda that includes multimodal robustness and LLM-assisted ASR only under reliability controls such as source grounding, task-boundary clarity, hallucination checks, reproducibility, and compute reporting.

This framing is intended to make the review useful beyond any single method generation. As model families change, the central question remains whether the resource condition, language condition, adaptation choice, supervision source, evaluation protocol, and deployment constraint are aligned. That alignment is the review's main analytical contribution.

## 12.8 Section Takeaway

Section 12 has synthesized the review's evidence into one cross-block claim: reliable low-resource ASR depends on cross-layer alignment, not model scale alone. Foundation speech models improve the starting point, but persistent bottlenecks remain in data documentation, language fit, adaptation comparability, pseudo-label reliability, evaluation, robustness, reproducibility, compute, AVSR coverage, and constrained LLM assistance.

Section 13 should now convert these bottlenecks into a concrete research agenda. That agenda should be reliable, reproducible, compute-aware, dialect-aware, multimodal where justified, and cautious about LLM-assisted ASR. It should preserve the paper's core rule: future methods should be judged by whether they make low-resource ASR more trustworthy under real resource, language, and deployment conditions.
