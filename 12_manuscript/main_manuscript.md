# Automatic Speech Recognition for Low-Resource Languages in the Foundation-Model Era: Resources, Adaptation, Evaluation, and Multimodal Robustness

## Abstract

Low-resource and underrepresented-language automatic speech recognition (ASR) remains unreliable despite progress in end-to-end modeling, self-supervised learning, multilingual pretraining, weak supervision, and speech foundation models. These models have changed the starting point of low-resource ASR by allowing systems to begin from broad pretrained acoustic, multilingual, or speech-text representations rather than from small task-specific corpora. However, this shift has not removed the core reliability problem. Recognition quality still depends on data quality, validation coverage, dialect/language mismatch, orthographic normalization, domain/channel conditions, adaptation, pseudo-label and knowledge-distillation reliability, evaluation design, reproducibility, compute, robustness, multimodal constraints, and AVSR, SpeechLM, or LLM-assisted risks. This article presents a structured critical review with systematic mapping elements, not a fully exhaustive PRISMA-style systematic review, of low-resource ASR in the foundation-model era. It synthesizes evidence across resources and benchmarks, model families, data-centric methods, adaptation strategies, pseudo-labeling and distillation, evaluation, robustness, multimodal ASR, and LLM-assisted directions. The review proposes a six-layer taxonomy organized around resource conditions, language conditions, model families, adaptation methods, supervision strategies, and evaluation settings. It further connects this taxonomy to a synthesis of data-centric and model-centric solutions, a critical review of adaptation and weak-supervision reliability, and a future agenda for dialect-aware evaluation, reproducible benchmarking, compute-efficient adaptation, multimodal robustness, and bounded LLM-assisted post-processing. The central conclusion is that foundation models are a new starting point for low-resource ASR, not an endpoint: reliable recognition for underrepresented languages requires coordinated progress across resources, language-aware design, model adaptation, supervision quality, evaluation, robustness, reproducibility, and deployment constraints.

## 1. Introduction

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) has advanced rapidly, but the benefits of that progress remain unevenly distributed across languages, dialects, domains, and speech communities. High-resource languages now benefit from mature benchmarks, large transcribed corpora, robust toolkits, and increasingly capable pretrained systems. Many underrepresented languages still face a different reality: limited validated speech, fragmented or inconsistent transcripts, scarce metadata, weak benchmark coverage, dialect imbalance, noisy channels, and constrained deployment settings. The gap is not only a matter of model accuracy. It is a gap in resources, evaluation infrastructure, reproducibility, and the ability to know whether a system will work reliably for the speakers and conditions it is meant to serve [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey; @ardila2020commonvoice; @conneau2022fleurs; @shi2023mlsuperb].

The foundation-model era has changed the technical starting point for this problem. Earlier low-resource ASR work often assumed that a new language required a largely task-specific recognition pipeline, careful lexicon or acoustic modeling, and enough transcribed speech to make supervised training viable. Self-supervised speech representation learning, multilingual pretraining, weakly supervised recognition, and speech foundation models have altered that assumption. Systems based on wav2vec 2.0, XLS-R, Whisper, MMS, SeamlessM4T, and newer speech-language models show that ASR research can now begin from broad pretrained acoustic, multilingual, or speech-text representations rather than from isolated small corpora [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms; @seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt; @tian25b_interspeech]. The relevant question is now how to adapt, validate, and deploy such systems under target-language constraints.

This changed starting point also changes how evidence should be interpreted. A result that would once have demonstrated the feasibility of training a recognizer may now demonstrate only that a pretrained system can be adapted under a particular data, language, and evaluation condition.

This shift should not be confused with a solution. Foundation models can improve baseline performance and expand language coverage, but they do not remove the conditions that make ASR low-resource. A language may have some transcribed hours but weak validation data; public speech may lack dialect labels, transcript policy, or reliable licensing; a multilingual model may include related languages but still fail under a target dialect, script, channel, or domain; and a model may improve pooled WER while hiding subgroup failures. Orthographic inconsistency, code-switching, morphology, domain mismatch, noisy speech, scarce compute, and deployment constraints still shape adaptation strategy, pseudo-label reliability, benchmark comparability, and deployment risk.

The central argument of this review is therefore balanced. Foundation speech models can reduce the labeled-data burden or provide stronger initial baselines, but they can also amplify hidden resource problems by masking dialect imbalance, normalizing away valid forms, relying on high-resource language priors, or producing fluent but unsupported output. LLM-assisted correction, rescoring, contextual biasing, post-ASR normalization, SpeechLM systems, and audio-visual ASR extend the design space, but they also introduce risks around hallucination, over-correction, task boundary confusion, modality mismatch, privacy, compute opacity, and reproducibility [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper; @shi2022avhubert; @anwar23_interspeech].

For this reason, the review defines low-resource ASR as a multidimensional condition rather than a simple label for few transcribed hours. Labeled data scarcity remains important, but it is only one layer. Low-resource status may also arise from weak validation sets, poor metadata, limited unlabeled or weakly labeled speech, dialect and accent imbalance, inconsistent writing conventions, script and normalization complexity, domain mismatch, noisy channels, code-switching, morphological variation, limited compute, restricted access, or deployment contexts that differ sharply from benchmark conditions. This broader definition matters because method choice depends on the bottleneck. Corpus filtering, normalization, augmentation, continued pretraining, parameter-efficient adaptation, pseudo-labeling, knowledge distillation, AVSR, or LLM-assisted correction are not interchangeable solutions; each is useful only under specific resource, language, supervision, and evaluation conditions.

A new review is needed because the field has outgrown older categories. Current work spans low-resource ASR surveys, end-to-end ASR, self-supervised speech learning, multilingual benchmarks, data-centric corpus construction, parameter-efficient adaptation, pseudo-labeling and distillation, fairness and robustness evaluation, audio-visual speech recognition, SpeechLMs, and LLM-assisted ASR. Treating these strands independently risks missing the foundation-model-era problem: progress in one layer often depends on constraints in another. A strong multilingual model still needs transparent resources and target-language validation; a data-centric pipeline still depends on model coverage and evaluation design; pseudo-labeling and LLM-assisted correction still need reliability controls.

The review also responds to a practical interpretability problem. Low-resource ASR papers often report improvements under different assumptions about data volume, transcript policy, base model, adaptation budget, decoding setup, validation split, or scoring convention. Without a shared taxonomy, it is difficult to tell whether a gain comes from the model family, corpus, adaptation method, pseudo-labeling pipeline, normalization rule, evaluation set, or compute used. Broad multilingual averages also do not necessarily show reliability for a particular language variety, noisy channel, orthographic convention, or deployment setting. The review therefore treats taxonomy, evidence mapping, and evaluation critique as central contributions rather than supporting apparatus.

This review addresses the following main review question: how have foundation speech models changed low-resource ASR, and what resource, adaptation, evaluation, and robustness challenges still prevent reliable recognition for underrepresented languages? Six supporting questions structure the analysis. First, what does "low-resource" mean in ASR beyond limited labeled hours? Second, how have self-supervised, weakly supervised, multilingual, and foundation speech models changed low-resource ASR? Third, which data-centric strategies remain necessary in the foundation-model era? Fourth, which adaptation strategies are most effective for low-resource ASR, and under what conditions? Fifth, how reliable are pseudo-labeling and knowledge distillation for low-resource ASR? Sixth, how should low-resource ASR be evaluated and extended toward robust, multimodal, and LLM-assisted systems?

The review makes four contributions. First, it proposes a foundation-model-era taxonomy of low-resource ASR organized by resource condition, language condition, model family, adaptation method, supervision strategy, and evaluation setting. This taxonomy treats low-resource ASR as an interaction among evidence layers rather than as a list of model families. Second, it synthesizes data-centric and model-centric solutions, showing how corpus quality, normalization, filtering, augmentation, validation, self-supervised learning, multilingual transfer, and foundation models interact. Third, it critically reviews adaptation, pseudo-labeling, and knowledge distillation, with attention to parameter-efficient tuning, continued pretraining, teacher reliability, confidence filtering, teacher disagreement, and the risk that generated supervision can transfer errors into the student model. Fourth, it develops a future research agenda for reliable low-resource ASR, covering dialect-aware evaluation, reproducible benchmarking, compute-efficient adaptation, multimodal and audio-visual robustness, and bounded LLM-assisted correction, rescoring, contextual biasing, and post-ASR normalization.

The scope is field-level. Individual languages and regional settings matter because they reveal concrete interactions among data scarcity, dialect variation, script and orthographic policy, noise, and evaluation design. However, this review does not use any one language as a case-study contribution or organize the paper around a single prior project. Its object is the broader research problem: how to evaluate low-resource ASR when modern speech models, data pipelines, adaptation strategies, weak supervision, multimodal inputs, and language-model assistance interact. This boundary is important because a review of the field must distinguish illustrative examples from general claims, and must avoid letting a successful result in one language, dataset, or benchmark stand in for reliability across underrepresented languages.

Methodologically, the article is a structured critical review with systematic mapping elements rather than a fully exhaustive PRISMA-style systematic review. It uses documented search logs, inclusion and exclusion criteria, citation-verification labels, a Core 60 reference backbone, evidence-to-claim mapping, and cross-block synthesis matrices to control the relationship between sources and manuscript claims. This structure is important because the evidence base contains different levels of maturity. Peer-reviewed and primary-source-verified work carries the central claims. Secondary-source and watchlist evidence is used cautiously, especially for fast-moving 2025-2026 topics such as SpeechLM systems, contextual ASR, LLM-assisted correction, hallucination evaluation, and emerging multimodal methods. The goal is not to claim universal coverage of all ASR literature, but to provide an auditable synthesis of the evidence needed to evaluate low-resource ASR in the foundation-model era.

The manuscript is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the review methodology and synthesis controls. Section 3 defines low-resource ASR beyond labeled hours, Section 4 reviews the transition to foundation speech models, Section 5 examines resources and benchmarks, and Section 6 introduces the six-layer taxonomy. Sections 7-9 synthesize data-centric strategies, adaptation methods, pseudo-labeling, and knowledge distillation. Sections 10-11 discuss evaluation, robustness, multimodal ASR, SpeechLMs, and LLM-assisted ASR. Sections 12-14 provide the cross-block synthesis, future agenda, and conclusion.

## 2. Review Methodology and Search Protocol

### 2.1 Review Design

This article is designed as a structured critical review with systematic mapping elements. This framing is deliberate. Low-resource ASR in the foundation-model era is not confined to a single model family, dataset, or benchmark tradition. It spans resource creation, multilingual corpora, self-supervised and weakly supervised speech models, adaptation strategies, pseudo-labeling, knowledge distillation, evaluation protocols, robustness, multimodal ASR, and LLM-assisted correction or rescoring. A narrow method survey would miss the interactions among these layers, while a fully exhaustive systematic review would require a complete PRISMA-style retrieval and screening flow that is not claimed here.

The methodological goal is therefore twofold. First, the review records search sources, eligibility criteria, screening decisions, citation-verification status, and evidence controls so that the process remains auditable. Second, it synthesizes the literature into a foundation-model-era taxonomy, evidence-to-claim map, Core 60 citation backbone, cross-block synthesis layer, and future research agenda. The review is not organized as a paper-by-paper narrative. It is organized around controlled synthesis: what each source can support, how strongly it has been verified, which research question it informs, and where its limitations constrain the manuscript claim.

Eligibility was defined by the locked research questions and by the review's field-level scope. Work focused on a single language or project-specific material was not used to define the central scope unless it illustrated a broader low-resource ASR issue such as normalization, dialect imbalance, pseudo-label quality, noisy speech, or multimodal robustness. The review is therefore field-level rather than language- or project-centered.

## 3. What Makes ASR Low-Resource?

Low-resource ASR is often described as a shortage of transcribed speech, but labeled-hour counts are only the most visible part of the problem. Labeled speech remains essential: it supports supervised adaptation, calibrates decoding behavior, and supplies the development and test sets through which recognition quality is measured. Yet the same number of transcribed hours can represent very different scientific conditions depending on who was recorded, how speech was elicited, how transcripts were normalized, whether the data match the intended deployment domain, and whether evaluation metadata expose dialect, channel, and speaker variation. In this review, low-resource ASR is therefore treated as a multidimensional reliability condition: the available resources are insufficiently complete, balanced, validated, documented, or reproducible to support robust recognition for the target language community and use case.

This broader definition is consistent with earlier low-resource ASR work. Under-resourced ASR was already framed as an interaction among acoustic resources, pronunciation resources, text resources, language modeling, and evaluation design rather than as a single threshold of labeled hours [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey]. Babel and OpenASR-style evaluations similarly made resource packs, constrained training data, and comparable evaluation part of the task definition [@gales2014_babel_project_research_cued; @peterson2022_openasr21]. More recent multilingual benchmark work extends the same logic: a language's presence in a benchmark does not by itself show that the benchmark captures the dialectal, orthographic, demographic, or domain conditions that determine reliable ASR [@chen2025mlsuperb2challenge].

The practical consequence is that "low-resource" should be read as a statement about evidence quality, not only data quantity. Ten hours of clean read speech with stable orthography and matched evaluation data do not create the same modeling problem as ten hours of conversational, noisy, dialectally diverse speech with inconsistent transcripts. Likewise, a large multilingual corpus may still be low-resource for a particular variety, speaker group, script convention, or deployment domain if that condition is poorly represented. This is why the review begins with the resource condition before turning to model history: foundation speech models change what can be transferred into a low-resource setting, but they do not determine whether the target setting has enough reliable evidence for adaptation and evaluation.

The resource layer of low-resource ASR includes labeled, unlabeled, weakly labeled, pseudo-labeled, multilingual, and multimodal evidence. These resource types are not interchangeable. Labeled speech supports supervised adaptation and evaluation, but it can be narrow, inconsistently transcribed, or weakly documented. Unlabeled speech supports self-supervised learning or continued pretraining, but it does not validate recognition behavior. Weakly labeled and web-mined speech can provide scale, while subtitles, crawled transcripts, segmentation, language identification, and source heterogeneity can introduce systematic noise. Pseudo-labeled speech expands supervision through teacher models, yet its value depends on teacher reliability, confidence filtering, calibration, and domain match. Multilingual corpora support transfer, but they can overrepresent high-resource or well-standardized languages while leaving target varieties thin. Multimodal and audio-visual resources can improve robustness under noise, but they introduce collection, alignment, privacy, and coverage constraints.

Large public datasets show why these distinctions matter. Common Voice and VoxPopuli expanded multilingual speech resources and made many cross-language experiments more feasible [@ardila2020commonvoice; @wang2021voxpopuli]. However, dataset size without validation, metadata, licensing, source information, and dialect coverage is not enough. Dataset-quality work makes this concern concrete by showing that multilingual speech resources can contain transcript, audio, silence, and planning problems that affect fairness and reproducibility [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. A corpus can therefore be valuable and still be incomplete evidence for a low-resource ASR claim.

Foundation models sharpen rather than remove this resource problem. Self-supervised and multilingual models such as wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, WavLM, and XLS-R show how unlabeled and multilingual speech can be converted into transferable representations [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @hsu2021hubert; @chen2022wavlm; @babu22_interspeech]. Weakly supervised and massively multilingual systems such as Whisper and MMS further show that large-scale supervision can create strong starting points for recognition across many languages [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. But these models still require target-language evidence to reveal errors, validate transcript conventions, compare dialects and domains, and judge whether transfer is reliable. Foundation models move the bottleneck from initial model training toward data quality, adaptation, and evaluation; they do not turn incomplete local evidence into complete local reliability.

Low-resource ASR is also shaped by language conditions that affect both modeling and scoring. Dialect variation changes acoustic patterns, pronunciations, vocabulary, and code choice within a named language. Orthographic inconsistency changes the relation between speech and written reference text. Code-switching changes tokenization, language modeling, and error interpretation. Morphology can create large surface-form variation from limited lexical material. Script complexity can make spacing, diacritics, punctuation, casing, and normalization choices part of the ASR task rather than a cosmetic post-processing step. A model may fail because a dialect, accent, or code-switched pattern is absent from training data; a score may be misleading because the reference transcript normalizes one spelling convention while the model outputs another plausible convention.

Benchmark and dataset evidence supports this interpretation. IndicSUPERB and ML-SUPERB-style benchmarks show that language-family and regional coverage matter for multilingual evaluation [@javed2023indsuperb; @shi2023mlsuperb]. AfriSpeech-200 makes accent and domain metadata central to understanding performance across speaker populations [@olatunji2023_afrispeech200]. The ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge extends this logic by making language varieties and inclusive ASR benchmarking explicit evaluation concerns [@chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. Pashto can be used as a brief illustrative example of dialect variation, transcript normalization, and script consistency, but the field-level claim is broader: a language is low-resource when its available resources fail to represent the linguistic variation that recognition and scoring must handle.

Domain and channel mismatch add another layer of fragility. Read speech, broadcast speech, telephone speech, meetings, clinical speech, classroom speech, conversational speech, and web audio differ in speaking style, channel, noise, overlap, segmentation, privacy constraints, and transcription convention. A system that performs well on a clean benchmark can fail when moved to noisy telephony, local broadcast, dialect-rich conversation, or far-field meeting speech. Multilingual benchmarks such as FLEURS, XTREME-S, ML-SUPERB, and ML-SUPERB 2.0 improve comparability, but they cannot cover every deployment domain, channel, dialect, or orthographic condition [@conneau2022fleurs; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @shi2024mlsuperb2]. Corpus evidence points in the same direction: large public resources can improve coverage while still requiring source-aware validation and metadata to support reliable claims [@ardila2020commonvoice; @wang2021voxpopuli; @lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech].

Evaluation must therefore be multidimensional as well. WER and CER are necessary because they provide standard summaries of recognition error, but pooled scores can hide the same weaknesses that define a low-resource setting: language variety, domain, transcript normalization, speaker population, robustness, reproducibility, and compute cost. ML-SUPERB, XTREME-S, FLEURS, IndicSUPERB, AfriSpeech-200, and ML-SUPERB 2.0 broaden coverage, but their results still need to be interpreted through metadata, normalization, and reporting conditions [@shi2023mlsuperb; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @conneau2022fleurs; @javed2023indsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. Evaluation should also document transcript normalization, data-quality controls, cost, and reproducibility because these factors can masquerade as differences in ASR quality [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech; @yang2021superb; @feng2022superbslt].

Low-resource ASR is therefore a multidimensional reliability condition rather than a simple shortage of labeled hours. Scarce labeled speech, weak validation, noisy or weakly labeled resources, teacher-generated supervision, dialect and domain mismatch, orthographic inconsistency, code-switching, morphology, script complexity, fragile benchmarks, and limited compute or deployment capacity all affect whether a system can be trusted. This definition creates the bridge to Section 4: foundation speech models improve transfer and initialization, but they do not erase the constraints that make ASR low-resource in the first place.

## 4. From Hybrid ASR to Foundation Speech Models

Before the foundation-model era, low-resource ASR was difficult not only because labeled speech was scarce, but because the surrounding expertise and resource infrastructure were also scarce. Hybrid ASR systems separated acoustic modeling, pronunciation lexicons, language modeling, decoding, and adaptation into distinct components. This modularity made the engineering problem interpretable, but it also meant that a low-resource language often needed expert-built lexicons, pronunciation rules, transcribed speech, text corpora, and task-specific alignments before competitive recognition could be attempted. Babel made this dependence visible: the task was not merely to fit a model, but to build enough acoustic, lexical, and evaluation infrastructure for constrained recognition and keyword spotting [@gales2014_babel_project_research_cued]. Earlier reviews similarly framed under-resourced ASR as a problem of incomplete language, speech, and evaluation resources rather than as a single missing dataset [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey].

End-to-end ASR changed the architecture but did not remove the resource problem. By learning a more direct mapping from acoustic sequences to output tokens, E2E systems reduced some hand-engineered pipeline dependencies and made neural sequence modeling central to ASR. However, E2E models still needed enough paired speech and text to learn target-language acoustics, grapheme or subword behavior, decoding conventions, and domain regularities. Modern E2E survey literature shows that E2E ASR consolidated many modeling choices, but still left data, adaptation, and evaluation as central design variables [@prabhavalkar2023_e2e_asr_survey]. For low-resource ASR, the pre-foundation baseline is therefore best understood as a setting where model training, lexicon design, text normalization, and evaluation were all constrained at once.

Self-supervised speech representation learning was the first major step in moving low-resource ASR away from dependence on large labeled corpora. SSL models use unlabeled speech to learn representations that can later be fine-tuned or evaluated on downstream tasks. Reviews of the SSL speech literature describe this as a broad shift in how speech systems exploit data: unlabeled audio becomes a training resource rather than unused background material [@mohamed2022_ssl_review]. wav2vec 2.0 is the clearest early anchor for this change because it showed that contrastive self-supervised pretraining could produce strong representations that improve ASR with limited labeled data [@baevski2020wav2vec2]. HuBERT, WavLM, w2v-BERT, random-projection quantizers, and data2vec broadened the representation-learning toolkit through masked prediction, robustness objectives, contrastive and masked modeling combinations, and alternative pretraining targets [@hsu2021hubert; @chen2022wavlm; @chung2021_w2vbert; @pmlr-v162-chiu22a; @pmlr-v162-baevski22a].

For low-resource ASR, the analytical consequence of SSL is more important than any individual objective: it became possible to start from reusable acoustic representations rather than from randomly initialized acoustic models. Yet SSL did not make low-resource ASR automatic. A pretrained encoder still needs a downstream ASR head, target-language or related-language adaptation, decoding choices, transcript normalization, and evaluation data. It helps with one resource condition, unlabeled speech, but does not supply dialect, orthographic, domain, or benchmark coverage.

Multilingual SSL extended the same logic across languages. XLSR-53 and XLS-R showed that cross-lingual pretraining could make representations more transferable, especially when target-language labeled data were limited [@conneau21_interspeech; @babu22_interspeech]. This was a major change for low-resource ASR because the initial model no longer had to be learned primarily from the target language. Instead, the target language could benefit from multilingual pretraining, related-language structure, and shared acoustic or phonetic patterns. Benchmarks such as ML-SUPERB then made multilingual ASR and language-identification evaluation more systematic across large language sets [@shi2023mlsuperb].

The same period saw weakly supervised systems reset practical expectations for baseline ASR. Whisper demonstrated that large-scale weak supervision could produce a strong multilingual ASR system with practical zero-shot and transfer value [@pmlr-v202-radford23a]. MMS pushed multilingual coverage further by scaling speech technology across a much larger language set [@pratap2024mms]. These systems changed what a low-resource project can reasonably start from: a new language may now begin with a pretrained multilingual or weakly supervised recognizer rather than with a small supervised model trained from scratch. However, multilingual scale should not be confused with uniform reliability. Language inclusion does not ensure balanced data, comparable transcript conventions, dialect coverage, or deployment-domain coverage. ML-SUPERB 2.0 and the ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge are important because they move evaluation toward modeling constraints, language varieties, and inclusive ASR benchmarking rather than relying only on broad language counts [@shi2024mlsuperb2; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge].

The foundation-model framing broadens ASR beyond acoustic encoders and multilingual recognizers. SpeechT5 and SpeechUT are useful transitional examples because they connect speech representation learning with encoder-decoder and speech-text pretraining designs [@ao2022speecht5; @zhang2022speechut]. SpeechLM-style work extends this direction by integrating speech and unpaired text more explicitly [@zhang2024_speechlm]. Speech-text and multimodal systems expand the model space further. SeamlessM4T frames speech and text tasks jointly across many languages, including ASR-related capability, speech translation, and multilingual speech-text modeling [@seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt]. OpusLM provides an open SpeechLM example with staged training and speech-language modeling evidence [@tian25b_interspeech].

The boundary condition is important. Speech-language and speech-text systems should not be treated as automatic evidence that low-resource ASR is solved. Translation-centric systems must be used carefully when making ASR claims, and early SpeechLM evidence should be linked to ASR-specific evaluation where possible. The Kanyen'keha foundation-model evaluation shows both sides of the Section 4 argument: speech foundation models can materially improve the low-resource starting point, but performance still depends on adaptation choices, language resources, and evaluation conditions [@geng25c_interspeech]. LLM-assisted correction and rescoring belong mainly to Section 11; here they are best treated as a future-facing extension of the foundation-model trajectory, not as the main account of ASR model evolution.

The analytical core of this section is that foundation speech models improve the starting point of low-resource ASR, but they shift the bottleneck toward adaptation, data quality, and evaluation rather than eliminating it. SSL models make unlabeled speech useful; multilingual SSL improves transfer and initialization; weakly supervised and massively multilingual systems create stronger baselines and broader coverage; and speech-language or speech-text systems expand the architectural space. What remains is the need for target-language evidence, transcript-normalization decisions, adaptation strategies that fit data and compute constraints, and benchmark interpretation through language coverage, source quality, and evaluation protocol.

## 5. Resources and Benchmarks

Resources and benchmarks determine whether foundation-model progress can be interpreted. Strong pretrained models make resource quality more important, not less important, because a better starting model can make weak benchmarks look more conclusive than they are. If a foundation model performs well on an aggregate test set, the result is meaningful only if the dataset captures the relevant language varieties, transcript conventions, domains, channels, speaker populations, and evaluation protocols.

Earlier low-resource ASR had to build many elements from the ground up: transcribed speech, lexicons, language models, and benchmark splits [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey; @gales2014_babel_project_research_cued]. Modern systems can often begin from multilingual or weakly supervised pretrained models, but those models still need reliable evidence that they work for the intended language and setting. Resource and benchmark design therefore become the mechanism through which claims about foundation-model progress are tested rather than assumed. A resource is not valuable only because it is large. It is valuable when its provenance, supervision type, metadata, validation procedure, access conditions, and evaluation role are clear enough to support interpretable ASR claims.

ASR resources differ by what they enable and by the risks they introduce. Labeled corpora support supervised adaptation and evaluation, but they can be small, domain-narrow, dialectally skewed, or inconsistently normalized. Unlabeled corpora support self-supervised learning and continued pretraining, but they cannot validate recognition behavior without downstream references. Weakly labeled, web-mined, and pseudo-labeled data can provide scale, but source noise, transcript mismatch, language-identification errors, teacher-model errors, and filtering choices shape what the model learns. Multilingual resources support transfer and comparison but may hide imbalance across languages, scripts, domains, and speaker communities. Multimodal and audio-visual resources can support robustness under noise or ambiguity, but they require aligned modalities and often have narrower language and domain coverage than audio-only corpora.

The evidence base contains several dataset and benchmark families that illustrate how resource design shapes low-resource ASR claims. Common Voice is a major crowdsourced multilingual speech resource and is important because it links openness, community contribution, validation, and multilingual coverage [@ardila2020commonvoice]. Babel and OpenASR-style resources represent a different tradition: constrained low-resource challenge settings where language packs, limited training material, and comparable evaluation protocols are part of the research design [@gales2014_babel_project_research_cued; @peterson2022_openasr21]. MLS and VoxPopuli expand multilingual speech resources through large-scale corpus construction and unlabeled or semi-supervised use cases, but their domains and supervision conditions still shape what they can prove [@pratap20_interspeech; @wang2021voxpopuli].

Benchmark families such as FLEURS, XTREME-S, ML-SUPERB, ML-SUPERB 2.0, and the ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge extend comparison from individual corpora to multilingual evaluation protocols [@conneau2022fleurs; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @shi2023mlsuperb; @shi2024mlsuperb2; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. Their value is not only language count, but shared tasks, splits, model constraints, and reporting expectations. Their limitation is the risk of treating broad language coverage as dialect coverage, domain coverage, script comparability, or deployment readiness. IndicSUPERB, AfriSpeech-200, and foundation-model-era data-pipeline work add regional, accent, domain, metadata, and preprocessing evidence to this benchmark picture [@javed2023indsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200; @tian24_interspeech].

Dataset size and language count are weak evidence without quality controls. Low-resource ASR often depends on community contribution, public media, parliamentary speech, audiobooks, clinical speech, web audio, or challenge packs, each with different recording conditions, speaker demographics, transcript conventions, licensing, and validation. A dataset with many hours may still be fragile if transcripts are noisy, metadata are thin, dialect distribution is unknown, or validation is inconsistent. Multilingual dataset audit evidence turns this concern into concrete ASR risk by linking transcript problems, audio problems, silence, and planning failures to sociolinguistic awareness and proactive language planning [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech].

Benchmark comparability is difficult because ASR datasets differ along many axes at once. Two benchmarks may report WER or CER, but use different domains, scripts, tokenization conventions, punctuation rules, casing, normalization pipelines, train/dev/test splits, or speaker metadata. In low-resource ASR, these differences are not minor implementation details. They can change whether a reported gain reflects better recognition, better normalization alignment, easier domain conditions, or more favorable language coverage. Orthography-aware evaluation evidence makes the same point at the scoring level: transcript conventions and naturally occurring spelling variation can change measured error rates, so normalization rules must be explicit [@karita2023lenient]. Robustness benchmarks such as CHiME-7 further show that clean read-speech evaluation does not cover far-field, multi-device, meeting, or overlap conditions that matter in deployed systems [@cornell2023chime7].

The foundation-model era increases this risk because stronger models encourage broader comparisons. A multilingual or weakly supervised model can be run on many languages, but the resulting table may hide weak performance on particular varieties, domains, scripts, or speaker groups. Benchmark comparison should therefore report not only aggregate accuracy, but also the resource condition, validation status, metadata availability, normalization protocol, model and decoding setup, and compute or deployment constraints. Resources and benchmarks are not passive inputs to model development. They are the evidentiary infrastructure that connects model claims to language communities and deployment settings.

## 6. Foundation-Model-Era Taxonomy of Low-Resource ASR

Sections 3-5 establish the need for a taxonomy specific to the foundation-model era. Section 3 defined low-resource ASR as a multidimensional reliability condition rather than a simple shortage of labeled hours. Section 4 showed that foundation speech models change the starting point by making transfer, weak supervision, and multilingual pretraining more powerful. Section 5 showed that resource and benchmark quality determine whether those model gains can be interpreted. The taxonomy in this section is the review's organizing contribution: it connects resource conditions, language conditions, model families, adaptation methods, supervision strategies, and evaluation settings into a single framework.

This taxonomy is not a generic ASR taxonomy. A generic taxonomy could list hybrid systems, end-to-end systems, self-supervised encoders, multilingual recognizers, and speech-language models as successive model families. That would repeat Section 4 and miss the central low-resource problem. In the foundation-model era, the crucial question is not only which model family is used, but how that model interacts with available resources, language variation, adaptation constraints, supervision reliability, and evaluation design. Foundation models may provide stronger initialization, but their success still depends on data quality, language fit, adaptation choices, and benchmark interpretation [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. Similarly, multilingual benchmarks improve comparability, but they do not remove the need to report language, dialect, domain, and normalization conditions [@shi2023mlsuperb; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge].

The first layer is the resource layer. It describes what evidence is available for training, adaptation, validation, and evaluation. Labeled speech supports supervised learning and test-set construction, but it can be narrow, inconsistently normalized, or weakly documented. Unlabeled speech supports self-supervised learning and continued pretraining, but it cannot by itself verify recognition quality. Weakly labeled or web-mined speech can provide scale, while also introducing source noise, segmentation errors, language-identification problems, and transcript mismatch. Pseudo-labeled resources can expand supervision, but they depend on teacher reliability, filtering, and calibration. Multilingual corpora support transfer and comparison, but their usefulness depends on coverage, balance, metadata, and domain. Multimodal or audio-visual resources can improve robustness, but they add alignment, privacy, and coverage constraints [@ardila2020commonvoice; @wang2021voxpopuli; @pratap20_interspeech; @lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech].

The second layer is the language layer. It captures the fact that languages are not homogeneous labels. Dialect variation affects acoustic patterns, pronunciation, vocabulary, and code choice. Orthographic inconsistency affects how speech is represented in reference transcripts and how errors are counted. Code-switching changes tokenization and language modeling. Morphology can increase surface-form variation and complicate word-level scoring. Script complexity affects segmentation, diacritics, spacing, punctuation, and normalization. Language-family mismatch affects whether representations learned from high-resource or related languages transfer well. Benchmarks such as ML-SUPERB 2.0, the ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge, IndicSUPERB, and AfriSpeech-200 make parts of this problem visible by focusing on language varieties, regional coverage, and accent or domain metadata [@shi2024mlsuperb2; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge; @javed2023indsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200]. Orthography-aware evaluation evidence further shows that transcript conventions and naturally occurring spelling variation can change measured error rates [@karita2023lenient].

The third layer is the model layer. Hybrid ASR systems represent the pre-foundation baseline in which acoustic models, lexicons, language models, and decoding were often engineered separately. End-to-end ASR reduced some pipeline boundaries but still depended on paired speech-text data and task-specific evaluation [@prabhavalkar2023_e2e_asr_survey]. Self-supervised speech encoders such as wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, and WavLM made unlabeled audio central to representation learning [@mohamed2022_ssl_review; @baevski2020wav2vec2; @hsu2021hubert; @chen2022wavlm]. Multilingual SSL and weakly supervised systems such as XLS-R, Whisper, and MMS changed the starting point for many low-resource settings by enabling stronger transfer and broader language coverage [@babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. Speech foundation models, speech-language models, and multimodal speech-text systems extend this layer beyond acoustic recognition alone, but translation-centric or early SpeechLM evidence should not be treated as proof that low-resource ASR is solved [@seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt; @tian25b_interspeech].

The fourth layer is the adaptation layer. It describes how a pretrained or multilingual model is made useful for a target language, domain, or deployment condition. Full fine-tuning updates the entire model and can be effective, but it may overfit scarce labeled data, require substantial compute, or harm existing multilingual capabilities. Continued pretraining can specialize a model using target or related unlabeled speech before supervised adaptation [@getman24_interspeech]. Adapter tuning, LoRA, and QLoRA represent parameter-efficient strategies that reduce trainable parameters or memory requirements, but their ASR evidence is uneven and should be separated from general NLP method evidence [@pmlr-v97-houlsby19a; @hu2022lora; @dettmers2023qlora]. Prompting, contextual biasing, transfer learning, and forgetting control also belong here, but they require safeguards against leakage, over-correction, semantic drift, and loss of prior multilingual capability [@udupa24_interspeech; @qian24_interspeech].

The fifth layer is the supervision layer. It distinguishes how learning signals are created and trusted. Supervised learning uses human-verified paired speech and text. Self-supervised learning uses unlabeled speech to learn representations. Self-training uses model-generated labels to retrain or adapt ASR systems. Pseudo-label filtering tries to decide which generated labels are reliable enough to use. Knowledge distillation transfers information from teacher models to student models, either from a single teacher or from multiple teachers. Agreement-based teacher selection treats teacher disagreement as a signal about label quality and risk. This layer is necessary because foundation models often blur the boundary between data and supervision: a pseudo-labeled corpus can appear to increase labeled data, but it may also amplify teacher errors [@kahn2019selftraining; @park20d_interspeech; @khurana2020dust; @leal21_interspeech; @farooq2023must; @ferraz2023multilingualdistilwhisper; @bhogale24_interspeech].

The sixth layer is the evaluation layer. It defines what counts as evidence of progress. WER and CER remain necessary, but they are not sufficient when used as pooled scores without information about orthography, dialect, domain, robustness, fairness, reproducibility, and compute. Orthography-aware evaluation addresses normalization, script, spelling, punctuation, and formatting. Dialect-wise and domain/channel-wise evaluation expose variation hidden by aggregate scores. Noise, far-field, and overlapping-speech robustness evaluate conditions beyond clean read speech. Fairness and demographic evaluation test whether performance differs by speaker population or recording provenance. Reproducibility and compute/deployment reporting determine whether methods can be validated and used outside large labs [@yang2021superb; @karita2023lenient; @cornell2023chime7; @koenecke2020racialdisparities; @liu2021casualconversations].

This layer also includes multimodal and future-facing evaluation concerns. AVSR robustness asks whether audio-visual information improves recognition under noisy or degraded audio conditions [@shi2022avhubert; @shi2022robustavsr; @anwar23_interspeech]. Hallucination and over-correction evaluation become important as ASR systems are connected to generative or LLM-assisted components; speech-to-text hallucination evidence shows why errors beyond WER can matter [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper]. These concerns are developed later in Sections 10 and 11, but they belong in the taxonomy because they define the boundary between promising system design and reliable evidence.

**Table 1. Low-resource ASR taxonomy.** This table maps the six taxonomy layers to representative subdimensions, what each layer captures, typical low-resource risks, and the review sections where each layer is developed.

The taxonomy should not be read as six independent boxes. Low-resource ASR failures often arise from interactions among layers. A strong foundation model may fail because its pretraining languages do not match the target language family, because the target corpus represents only one dialect, or because the test set uses a different orthographic convention. A pseudo-labeling pipeline may increase the amount of supervision while also reproducing teacher errors on noisy channels or underrepresented speakers. A multilingual benchmark may report an improved average score while hiding poor performance on a specific domain, variety, or script. A parameter-efficient adaptation method may reduce compute while producing a trade-off between new-language performance and forgetting.

The taxonomy's purpose is to make these interactions visible before the review turns to specific solution families. Resource conditions shape what data-centric strategies are needed. Language conditions determine how adaptation and evaluation should be interpreted. Model families define the available starting points. Adaptation methods decide how those starting points are specialized. Supervision strategies determine whether additional training signals are reliable. Evaluation settings decide whether the resulting claim is scientifically meaningful. In the foundation-model era, reliable low-resource ASR depends on the alignment of all six layers. The next section therefore turns to the first solution layer in detail: data-centric strategies.

## 7. Data-Centric Strategies in the Foundation-Model Era

Section 6 framed low-resource ASR as an interaction among resource, language, model, adaptation, supervision, and evaluation layers. The first practical consequence is that data-centric work remains central even when strong pretrained or foundation speech models are available. Foundation models can reduce the need to train recognizers from scratch, make unlabeled speech useful, and provide stronger zero-shot or few-shot baselines. They do not make target-language data reliable, representative, normalized, or evaluation-ready by themselves. In the foundation-model era, the data question therefore shifts from "how much data is available?" to "what kind of evidence does the data provide, and what risks does it introduce?"

This shift matters because model scale can amplify weak data as well as exploit strong data. Large weakly supervised and multilingual systems have changed baseline expectations for ASR across many languages [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. Yet heterogeneous speech-to-text training pipelines still depend on source selection, filtering, punctuation, truecasing, and label refinement [@tian24_interspeech]. Multilingual dataset audits likewise show that transcript, audio, silence, and sociolinguistic planning problems can affect reliability even in widely used resources [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. Data-centric strategy is therefore not just preprocessing. It is evidence control: corpus creation, validation, documentation, transcript normalization, segmentation, filtering, augmentation, metadata, and benchmark design determine what a later ASR claim can mean.

Corpus creation should be treated as a reliability pipeline rather than a collection count. A low-resource corpus often becomes training data, development data, benchmark evidence, and a community reference point at the same time. If it is narrow in speaker population, dialect, channel, domain, license, or transcript convention, later model comparisons inherit that narrowness. Common Voice illustrates the value of open multilingual collection and validation while also making language balance, accent coverage, and transcript quality central concerns [@ardila2020commonvoice]. Babel and OpenASR represent a different model of resource construction, where constrained language packs, conversational speech, and comparable evaluation define part of the research task [@gales2014_babel_project_research_cued; @peterson2022_openasr21]. MLS and VoxPopuli broaden the resource base through large-scale multilingual speech, but their domains and supervision conditions still constrain what their results can prove [@pratap20_interspeech; @wang2021voxpopuli].

Metadata is a data-centric strategy because it decides whether errors can be interpreted. Speaker, dialect, accent, domain, channel, collection source, and validation metadata make it possible to distinguish a general model failure from a subgroup, source, or domain failure. AfriSpeech-200 is important in this respect because accent and domain metadata are part of the dataset's value rather than background description [@olatunji2023_afrispeech200]. FLEURS, ML-SUPERB, ML-SUPERB 2.0, and related benchmarks improve multilingual comparison, but they do not remove the need to report source documentation, normalization choices, and subgroup behavior [@conneau2022fleurs; @shi2023mlsuperb; @shi2024mlsuperb2; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge]. A corpus can be valuable and still be incomplete evidence if its metadata do not reveal which language varieties, speakers, or domains are represented.

Transcript normalization is another core data intervention. Punctuation, casing, spelling variants, diacritics, spacing, numerals, script choices, and tokenization affect both the model's targets and the evaluation reference. In low-resource settings these conventions may be unstable, community-specific, or inherited from pipelines designed for other languages. A model trained on inconsistent transcripts learns a noisier target distribution; a model evaluated against inconsistently normalized references may be penalized for plausible variants rather than recognition failures. Dataset-quality work links transcript reliability to broader multilingual resource quality [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech], while speech-to-text foundation-model data evidence shows that punctuation and truecasing remain active design choices at scale [@tian24_interspeech]. Punctuation restoration and orthography-aware scoring evidence further show why formatting and spelling variation should be documented instead of hidden inside an evaluation script [@chen21d_interspeech; @karita2023lenient].

Filtering, segmentation, alignment, and language identification are equally consequential. Foundation-model-era ASR often uses mixtures of public corpora, web audio, subtitles, parliamentary recordings, audiobooks, challenge data, and weak transcripts. These sources differ in noise, overlap, speaker style, transcript timing, punctuation, casing, and domain. Filtering can reduce noise and make training more stable, but it can also remove difficult accents, noisy channels, code-switched utterances, minority dialects, or atypical speakers exactly because they are hard. Data-centric strategy must therefore balance cleanliness against representativeness. The best pipeline is not simply the one that produces the cleanest data; it is the one whose selection choices are documented and whose evaluation checks whether important varieties were excluded [@wang2021voxpopuli; @tian24_interspeech].

Augmentation remains useful, but it should not be mistaken for missing coverage. Speed perturbation and SpecAugment-style masking can expose models to controlled acoustic variation and reduce overfitting to small corpora [@ko15_interspeech; @park19e_interspeech]. These methods are strongest when they simulate plausible variation such as speaking rate, channel effects, reverberation, noise, or partial masking. They are weaker as substitutes for absent dialect, demographic, domain, or orthographic coverage. CHiME-style robustness evidence shows why distant, multi-device, overlapping, and noisy conditions require direct testing rather than assumptions from clean speech [@cornell2023chime7]. Audio-visual robustness work extends the same logic to cases where visual information may help under degraded audio, but those resources introduce their own coverage and alignment limits [@anwar23_interspeech].

Unlabeled, weakly labeled, and pseudo-labeled data connect data-centric strategy to Sections 8 and 9. Self-supervised and multilingual pretraining show why unlabeled speech matters: it can support transferable representations before task-specific labels exist [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @babu22_interspeech]. Weakly supervised systems show that large imperfect transcript mixtures can produce strong recognizers [@pmlr-v202-radford23a]. Pseudo-labeling can turn unlabeled speech into candidate supervision, but only after a teacher model, decoding setup, filtering rule, normalization policy, and validation protocol have shaped the labels [@park20d_interspeech; @khurana2020dust; @bhogale24_interspeech]. For Section 7, the point is bounded: generated labels are data artifacts requiring controls, not free replacements for verified human transcripts.

Data-centric strategy should therefore be selected from the failure mode. Scarce labeled speech calls for verified small corpora and careful split construction. Unlabeled target speech calls for SSL reuse, continued pretraining, or semi-supervised pipelines with target validation. Weak labels and web-mined data call for filtering, alignment, source documentation, and label refinement. Orthographic inconsistency calls for explicit transcript policy and raw/normalized scoring. Dialect, accent, domain, or channel mismatch calls for metadata-aware collection, balanced sampling, and subgroup reporting. Compute constraints call for smaller verified adaptation and evaluation subsets rather than unreported reliance on expensive models.

Data work remains a central scientific part of low-resource ASR. Foundation models change the model starting point, but the reliability of corpus sources, labels, metadata, normalization, filtering, augmentation, and evaluation design still determines what the system learns and what the benchmark can prove. Once these resource conditions are understood, the next question is how to adapt pretrained systems to the target language, domain, and compute setting.

## 8. Adaptation Strategies for Low-Resource ASR in the Foundation-Model Era

Adaptation is now the central model-side problem in foundation-model-era low-resource ASR. Earlier sections showed that modern systems often begin from pretrained SSL, multilingual, weakly supervised, or speech-language models rather than from recognizers trained entirely from scratch. That changes the starting point, but it does not answer which parts of the model should be updated, what evidence should guide updating, how much compute is acceptable, or how to detect failures after adaptation. The useful question is therefore not which adaptation method is best in general. It is which adaptation method fits the available labeled and unlabeled data, language condition, model family, compute budget, deployment domain, and evaluation target.

Full fine-tuning remains an important baseline. When a pretrained model already contains useful acoustic or multilingual representations and a small but reliable labeled target set is available, updating the full model can directly specialize the recognizer to target transcripts, acoustic conditions, and decoding behavior. XLSR-style and XLS-R evidence shows why multilingual SSL can provide a stronger starting point for low-resource fine-tuning [@conneau21_interspeech; @babu22_interspeech]. Whisper and MMS extend the same logic to weakly supervised and massively multilingual settings, where a low-resource project may begin from a strong recognizer rather than a randomly initialized model [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. Full fine-tuning is also a useful comparison point for more constrained methods because it shows what can be gained when many parameters may change.

The risk is that full fine-tuning can overfit exactly the narrow evidence available in a low-resource setting. A small labeled set may represent one dialect, recording channel, spelling convention, or domain. A model can improve an average WER while degrading a subgroup or previous multilingual capability. The compute burden can also make results difficult to reproduce. Benchmark work such as ML-SUPERB helps expose multilingual adaptation behavior, but its protocol still constrains what can be inferred [@shi2023mlsuperb]. Direct underrepresented-language evaluation, such as Kanyen'keha foundation-model ASR work, is valuable because it shows adaptation choices in a specific low-resource setting, but such evidence should not be generalized as a universal recipe [@geng25c_interspeech].

Continued pretraining is most relevant when labels are scarce but relevant unlabeled target-language, target-domain, or closely related speech exists. Instead of immediately fine-tuning on a small supervised set, the model is first exposed to additional speech that may better match the target acoustics, channel, style, or language variety. Continued pretraining for colloquial Finnish ASR provides a direct anchor for this strategy [@getman24_interspeech]. Speech-language-model work also shows that staged and continual pretraining can be part of broader speech-text development, although such evidence should remain bounded when the task is not purely low-resource ASR [@tian25b_interspeech]. The limitation is the same data-centric issue developed in Section 7: unlabeled data can be useful only when its source, quality, and domain relation are understood.

Parameter-efficient adaptation is attractive because many low-resource settings face memory, storage, deployment, or multi-language maintenance constraints. Adapters, LoRA, and QLoRA can reduce the number of trainable parameters or the memory needed for adaptation, and they can support modular language or domain variants [@pmlr-v97-houlsby19a; @hu2022lora; @dettmers2023qlora]. These method papers are important anchors, but they should not be cited as proof that PEFT is automatically best for low-resource ASR. Direct speech evidence should carry stronger ASR claims. Adapter pre-training and low-resource adapter tuning support adapter-based adaptation under low-resource and unseen-domain conditions [@udupa24_interspeech]. New-language adaptation and forgetting-control work shows why target gains, retention of previous languages, and trainable-parameter counts should be evaluated together [@qian24_interspeech].

Related-language and phonetic adaptation exploit the fact that a low-resource target language may share acoustic, phonological, script, or regional properties with languages better represented in pretraining or donor corpora. Cross-lingual SSL makes this transfer technically easier, and language-universal phonetic representation evidence supports phonetic-informed multilingual pretraining under suitable conditions [@feng23_interspeech]. The danger is false relatedness. A donor language may be typologically or geographically related while still mismatching the target in dialect, phonology, orthography, or domain. Transfer should therefore be justified with a donor-language rationale and verified with target-language evaluation rather than assumed from language names or broad family labels.

Prompting, contextual biasing, and decoder-side adaptation are more bounded. They may help when the main problem is domain vocabulary, named entities, terminology, code-switching cues, or output style rather than broad acoustic adaptation. Whisper-like and speech-language systems make such methods more visible because decoder behavior can sometimes be steered without full model updating [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @tian25b_interspeech]. However, contextual and generative components can leak information, over-correct valid outputs, insert unsupported content, or improve surface metrics while harming faithfulness. Speech-to-text hallucination evidence reinforces the need to evaluate errors beyond pooled WER when generative behavior enters the ASR pipeline [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper]. These methods should be treated as constrained adaptation tools, not mature replacements for data, fine-tuning, or validated ASR adaptation.

This comparison is deliberately conditional. A small reliable labeled set can justify full fine-tuning as a strong baseline, while compute or forgetting constraints may motivate adapters or LoRA-style methods. Relevant unlabeled target speech may justify continued pretraining, but only with source and domain reporting. A related donor language may help, but only if target-language evaluation confirms transfer. Contextual vocabulary problems may justify constrained prompting or biasing, but only with leakage, over-correction, and hallucination checks. In all cases, adaptation evidence should report the base model, trainable parameters, data sources, filtering, normalization, decoding setup, compute, and subgroup or domain breakdowns.

Forgetting and reproducibility make adaptation a multi-objective problem rather than a single accuracy optimization. Adding a new language to a multilingual ASR system may improve that language while degrading previous languages; preserving previous capability may reduce target specialization [@qian24_interspeech]. Toolkits and benchmark efforts support more reproducible speech experimentation, but a reported gain is interpretable only when the adaptation recipe is clear enough to repeat [@yang2021superb]. Low-resource ASR papers should therefore report not only WER or CER, but also what changed in the model, how data were prepared, how much compute was used, whether inference cost changed, and whether prior-language or prior-domain capability was checked.

Section 8 answers RQ4 by treating adaptation as a conditional design choice. Full fine-tuning, continued pretraining, PEFT, related-language transfer, contextual adaptation, and forgetting-aware adaptation each address different constraints and introduce different risks. The right method depends on labeled and unlabeled data, language relatedness, domain and channel match, transcript conventions, compute limits, deployment requirements, and evaluation design. The next question is how to expand supervision when verified paired speech and text remain scarce.

## 9. Pseudo-Labeling and Knowledge Distillation for Low-Resource ASR

Pseudo-labeling and knowledge distillation address the supervision layer of the taxonomy. Many low-resource settings have too little verified paired speech and text for stable supervised adaptation, but they may have unlabeled target speech, weak transcripts, web-mined audio, or access to stronger teacher models. These conditions make pseudo-labeling, self-training, and KD attractive because they can expand the training signal without manually transcribing every utterance. The central qualification is that supervision expansion is not automatically supervision improvement. A generated transcript becomes training data only after a teacher model, decoding setup, filtering rule, normalization policy, and validation protocol have shaped it.

Self-training uses an ASR model to generate labels for unlabeled speech and then trains or adapts a student using those generated labels. This can make target-language or target-domain speech useful when human labels are scarce. ASR self-training evidence shows that teacher quality, pseudo-label diversity, and training conditions affect whether the procedure helps [@kahn2019selftraining]. Noisy Student ASR further shows that filtering, balancing, augmentation, and student training design are part of the method rather than optional details [@park20d_interspeech]. Iterative pseudo-labeling can regenerate labels as the model improves, but repeated relabeling can also reinforce early mistakes when validation and filtering are weak [@xu2020iterativepseudolabeling; @likhomanenko2020slimipl].

Filtering is the analytical center of pseudo-labeling. Confidence filtering keeps labels that appear reliable according to model scores. Uncertainty filtering estimates instability through dropout disagreement or related signals. Evaluator-based or curriculum approaches rank labels before training. These methods matter because high confidence is not the same as correctness. A teacher can be confidently wrong under dialect mismatch, domain shift, noisy channels, code-switching, or orthographic variation. Uncertainty-driven self-training shows why selection rules can be central under domain mismatch [@khurana2020dust], and evidence on pretraining plus self-training reinforces that pseudo-labeling is most useful when integrated with representation learning and adaptation rather than treated as a shortcut [@xu2020selftraining].

Large-scale low-resource pseudo-labeling makes the reliability problem more explicit. When generated labels become a major source of supervision, selection rules, confidence measures, agreement signals, and validation sets determine whether the added data help or propagate errors. Recent low-resource pseudo-labeling evidence using multiple base models and evaluators supports the conservative claim that pseudo-label selection is a reliability control, not merely a scaling device [@bhogale24_interspeech]. Pseudo-labeling studies should therefore report teacher identity, decoding settings, normalization policy, confidence or uncertainty scores, thresholds, retained-data policy where possible, and validation behavior by language, domain, or channel.

Knowledge distillation is closely related but not identical. KD transfers information from a teacher model to a student through posterior distributions, decoded sequences, representations, or other training targets. The general method anchor is teacher-student distillation [@hinton2015distilling]. In low-resource ASR, KD can compress a large model, adapt a student to a target language, transfer multilingual knowledge, or use a foundation model as a supervision source. Intermediate KD evidence supports distinguishing hidden-state or intermediate representation transfer from output-only distillation [@yoon2022interkd]. Sequence-level KD overlaps with pseudo-labeling because the teacher's decoded sequence can become the student's training target; its risk is that a fluent or normalized sequence may still be wrong for the target speech.

Foundation-model teachers make KD more powerful and more risky. Whisper-like and MMS-like systems can provide stronger multilingual baselines and broader coverage than earlier teachers [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. Multilingual DistilWhisper-style evidence is relevant as a foundation-ASR distillation anchor, but the claim should remain cautious: a strong teacher can reduce the cost of initial transcription or support a smaller student, but it can also impose formatting conventions, normalize away valid variants, or perform unevenly across dialects and channels [@ferraz2023multilingualdistilwhisper]. A foundation-model teacher should therefore be treated as a candidate source of supervision, not as proof that labels are correct.

Multi-teacher KD and agreement-based selection try to reduce dependence on one teacher. If several plausible teachers agree on an utterance, that agreement may signal a more reliable label; if they disagree, the utterance may require rejection, lower weight, or manual review. Self-adaptive multilingual distillation shows how teacher and student confidence can be used in multilingual ASR distillation [@leal21_interspeech]. Multilingual student-teacher learning also shows that posterior mapping may be needed when teacher and student character sets differ [@farooq2023must]. These details are not implementation footnotes. Teacher identities, label spaces, tokenization, orthography, decoding style, and disagreement policy determine whether multi-teacher supervision is auditable.

These reliability controls are necessary because pseudo-labels are not equivalent to verified labels. Teacher errors can become training targets. Dialect or domain mismatch can make confidence poorly calibrated. Orthographic normalization can make generated labels look clean while changing what is learned and scored. Generative recognizers or speech-language components can produce fluent text that is not fully supported by the audio. LLM- or SpeechLLM-assisted pseudo-label refinement should remain future-facing and constrained until source-grounded correction, hallucination checks, semantic preservation, and target-language validation are reported.

Pseudo-labeling and KD also create evaluation obligations. Pooled WER or CER is not enough to show that generated supervision helped. A method may improve a global score while hurting a minority dialect, noisy channel, or orthographic convention. Studies should report the teacher model, teacher data source where known, decoding and normalization settings, filtering thresholds, amount of retained data where available, student architecture, KD objective, compute cost, and subgroup or domain results. For multi-teacher systems, they should report teacher agreement and disagreement handling. For foundation-model teachers, they should add checks for hallucination, over-normalization, and target-language mismatch.

Section 9 answers RQ5 by separating supervision expansion from supervision reliability. Pseudo-labeling, self-training, and KD can make low-resource ASR more feasible when verified labels are scarce, but their value depends on teacher quality, confidence, uncertainty, agreement, label normalization, language and domain match, and evaluation design. The next section therefore turns to evaluation, reproducibility, and robustness: the mechanisms needed to decide whether adaptation and generated supervision have produced real low-resource ASR progress rather than better-looking aggregate scores.

## 10. Evaluation, Reproducibility, and Robustness

Evaluation becomes more important, not less important, in the foundation-model era. Sections 3-9 show why: low-resource ASR is a multidimensional reliability problem; foundation models improve the starting point but do not remove resource and language mismatch; data-centric work determines what evidence is available; adaptation is conditional; and pseudo-labeling or KD can expand supervision while amplifying teacher errors. When average performance improves, the remaining failures may become less visible because they concentrate in particular languages, dialects, domains, scripts, speakers, channels, or generation behaviors. Evaluation is therefore part of the evidence chain, not merely a final score.

WER and CER remain necessary. ASR still needs standard error measures that allow models, corpora, and benchmark splits to be compared. The problem is that WER and CER are insufficient when pooling, averaging, tokenization, normalization, and subgroup conditions are hidden. A pooled WER can hide whether errors fall mainly on tail languages, minority dialects, a noisy channel, or a domain that motivated the system in the first place. CER can be useful for some scripts and languages, but it does not automatically solve word-boundary, morphology, punctuation, or normalization problems. Multilingual benchmark work such as ML-SUPERB, FLEURS, XTREME-S, ML-SUPERB 2.0, and the ML-SUPERB 2.0 Challenge provides necessary shared infrastructure, but it also shows why language coverage, constraints, and reporting protocols must be interpreted carefully [@shi2023mlsuperb; @conneau2022fleurs; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @shi2024mlsuperb2; @chen2025mlsuperb2challenge].

Orthography-aware evaluation is central for low-resource ASR because transcript conventions may be unstable, community-specific, or underdocumented. Spelling variation, diacritics, word spacing, casing, punctuation, script choice, numerals, morphology, and tokenization can all change measured error. Section 7 treated transcript normalization as part of the data pipeline; Section 9 showed that pseudo-labels can inherit teacher normalization. The evaluation consequence is that scoring rules must be reported. Raw scoring can show whether a model matches the reference convention; normalized scoring can show whether recognition remains useful after removing formatting differences. Neither is universally superior. Lenient and orthography-sensitive ASR evaluation evidence shows how naturally occurring spelling variation can distort error rates when acceptable variants are not recognized by the scorer [@karita2023lenient]. Dataset-quality and foundation-model data-pipeline evidence reinforces that transcript consistency, punctuation, and casing remain active reliability issues rather than cosmetic details [@lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech; @tian24_interspeech].

Language-, dialect-, domain-, and demographic-aware reporting is the second requirement. For multilingual ASR, language-wise and macro-averaged results can prevent high-resource or well-represented languages from dominating interpretation. For inclusive benchmarks, dialect, accent, and language-variety reporting can reveal whether the system serves the communities that motivated the work. For deployment, domain and channel reporting can show whether a model trained on clean read speech transfers to conversational, telephony, clinical, classroom, broadcast, or far-field speech. FLEURS, XTREME-S, ML-SUPERB, IndicSUPERB, AfriSpeech-200, and ML-SUPERB 2.0 all make parts of this reporting problem visible, while still having scope limits of their own [@conneau2022fleurs; @conneau2022xtreme_s; @shi2023mlsuperb; @javed2023indsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200; @shi2024mlsuperb2]. Fairness evidence from commercial ASR and demographic speech resources further shows that aggregate scores can hide systematic differences across speaker groups [@koenecke2020racialdisparities; @liu2021casualconversations].

Robustness evaluation is also essential because many low-resource use cases are not clean read-speech tasks. They involve mobile recordings, telephone speech, far-field microphones, overlapping speakers, noisy environments, code-switching, broadcast audio, clinical interaction, classroom speech, or fieldwork recordings. A system evaluated only on clean benchmark audio may look strong while failing in the setting where the technology is needed. CHiME-6 and CHiME-7 style evidence is useful here as robustness methodology: it addresses distant, multi-speaker, multi-device, meeting, and diarization-aware ASR conditions that clean evaluations do not expose [@watanabe2020chime6; @cornell2023chime7]. These benchmarks are not themselves proof of low-resource-language coverage; their value is that they demonstrate the kinds of scenario differences low-resource ASR evaluation should test.

Reproducibility and compute reporting are part of evaluation because foundation-model ASR can be difficult to interpret without the full recipe. Reports should state the base model and checkpoint, training and adaptation data, filtering rules, supervised and unsupervised data amounts where available, tokenization and normalization policy, decoding parameters, hardware, training cost, inference cost, trainable parameters, random seeds or variance where relevant, split construction, and scoring scripts. This matters especially for underrepresented languages because datasets may be small or restricted and large-model adaptation may be unaffordable for many communities or research groups. Toolkits such as ESPnet, SpeechBrain, and Kaldi provide reproducibility infrastructure, but infrastructure is not a substitute for reporting the exact experiment [@watanabe2018espnet; @ravanelli2021speechbrain; @povey2011kaldi]. Efficiency-oriented benchmark work reinforces that accuracy should be interpreted alongside cost and reporting completeness [@feng2022superbslt].

Generated or contextual ASR adds another evaluation layer. A speech-language model, generative recognizer, or LLM-assisted correction system can produce fluent text that is not fully supported by the audio. It may over-correct a dialect form, insert a plausible named entity, normalize away meaningful variation, or use context in a way that leaks the answer. Speech-to-text hallucination evidence shows why unsupported generated content must be evaluated beyond aggregate WER [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper]. Frontier work on contextual ASR and LLM-based correction can motivate emerging protocols, but it should remain bounded: these sources help identify hallucination, over-correction, semantic drift, and context-leakage risks rather than proving that LLM assistance is mature for low-resource ASR [@ma2024asrerrorcorrection; @koudounas2025shallow; @wang2025contextasrbench].

This checklist follows directly from Sections 3-9. If the problem is orthography, the protocol should document transcript conventions and raw or normalized scoring. If the problem is multilingual or dialectal coverage, the report should include language-wise, macro-averaged, dialect-wise, or accent-wise results where metadata allow. If the problem is adaptation, the report should identify the base model, trainable parameters, data sources, compute, decoding, and forgetting checks where relevant. If the problem is multimodal or LLM-assisted ASR, it should test modality mismatch, hallucination, over-correction, semantic drift, context leakage, and source grounding.

An adaptation method cannot be judged without knowing whether gains come from the base model, the target data, the trainable-parameter budget, or decoding. A pseudo-labeling method cannot be judged without knowing how teacher errors were selected or removed. Evaluation is therefore the mechanism that connects resources, adaptation, supervision, robustness, and deployment into a comparable claim.

The evaluation argument converts the review's reliability framing into reporting requirements. WER and CER remain necessary, but reliable low-resource ASR evaluation must expose orthographic, dialectal, domain, demographic, robustness, hallucination, reproducibility, and compute-related failures that aggregate scores can hide. Once systems move toward multimodal, SpeechLM, and LLM-assisted ASR, evaluation must also handle modality mismatch, correction drift, contextual bias, and compute opacity.

## 11. Multimodal, AVSR, and LLM-Assisted ASR

Multimodal, AVSR, SpeechLM, and LLM-assisted ASR directions respond to real limits of audio-only recognition: noisy channels, weak language modeling, dialect variation, unstable transcript conventions, limited supervision, and evaluation gaps. Adding visual streams, speech-text pretraining, or text-side language models can expand the design space, but it also expands the reliability problem. The central claim is therefore cautious: these directions are promising bounded extensions whose value depends on modality availability, task definition, source grounding, evaluation protocol, reproducibility, compute, and privacy.

Audio-visual speech recognition is best understood as a robustness strategy, not as a general solution to low-resource ASR. Audio-only systems degrade when speech is noisy, far-field, overlapped, reverberant, or recorded through poor channels. Visual speech cues can provide complementary information about articulation when the acoustic signal is degraded. AV-HuBERT and robust self-supervised AVSR evidence support the idea that audio-visual representation learning can improve robustness under degraded audio [@shi2022avhubert; @shi2022robustavsr]. MuAViC strengthens the low-resource relevance because it provides multilingual audio-visual ASR and speech-translation benchmark evidence rather than only narrow monolingual AVSR background [@anwar23_interspeech].

The constraints are substantial. Low-resource AVSR requires synchronized audio and video, visible articulators, reliable speaker-video alignment, enough language or variety coverage, and evaluation under both clean and degraded conditions. These requirements can be harder than collecting audio alone. Visual data also introduce consent, storage, privacy, lighting, camera, cultural, and accessibility concerns. AVSR may fail when lips are not visible, a speaker is off-camera, multiple speakers overlap, or the visual stream is desynchronized. The safe interpretation is conditional: AVSR may help when reliable visual streams exist and the main bottleneck is degraded audio; it is much less defensible when video is unavailable, inappropriate to collect, weakly aligned, or too expensive to process.

Multimodal speech-text foundation models broaden the space beyond conventional ASR. Systems such as SeamlessM4T connect speech recognition, speech translation, text translation, and multilingual speech-text modeling across many languages [@seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt]. Whisper and MMS provide broader context by showing how weakly supervised multilingual ASR and massively multilingual speech technology can change starting points and coverage [@pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms]. The task boundary is critical, however. ASR, speech translation, and post-ASR correction are not interchangeable. A model that performs well on speech translation does not automatically provide evidence for faithful source-language transcription. For a low-resource ASR review, speech-text systems should be cited as evidence that the model space is expanding, not as proof that ASR-specific resource, transcript, and evaluation problems have disappeared.

Speech-language models and Speech-LLM systems connect acoustic representations with text-side knowledge, instruction-like behavior, or broader language-model objectives. They may support ASR, speech understanding, translation, correction, rescoring, contextualization, or multitask generation. SpeechLM provides a useful speech-text pretraining anchor, while OpusLM gives a verified open SpeechLM example for staged speech-language modeling [@zhang2024_speechlm; @tian25b_interspeech]. These sources justify including SpeechLM systems in the taxonomy and future-facing discussion, but they do not justify a blanket claim that SpeechLMs reliably outperform ASR-specialized systems for low-resource languages. Their results still depend on language coverage, data provenance, connector or adapter design, decoding constraints, task prompts, compute, and ASR-specific evaluation.

LLM-assisted ASR is most defensible as bounded support for correction, rescoring, contextualization, or post-ASR normalization. An LLM can rescore N-best hypotheses or lattices, help select among plausible ASR outputs, support domain terminology or named entities, or enforce an explicit formatting policy. In each case, the assistance should remain constrained by acoustic evidence, ASR hypotheses, confidence information, or validated context. Unconstrained rewriting is different. A language model may replace a rare name with a common one, normalize a dialect form into a standard form, insert plausible content, or change a speaker's intended meaning. Surface WER may improve under some scoring conventions while source faithfulness worsens.

The evidence base supports risk-aware discussion rather than strong universal claims. Careless Whisper provides the Core 60 hallucination-risk anchor for evaluating unsupported generated content [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper]. Watchlist evidence on LLM-based ASR correction, hallucination benchmarks, and contextual ASR can motivate emerging safeguards, but it should not carry central claims that LLM correction is mature across low-resource settings [@ma2024asrerrorcorrection; @koudounas2025shallow; @wang2025contextasrbench]. The practical rule is traceability. If a system uses an N-best list or lattice, the report should show whether the final output remains grounded in acoustic hypotheses. If it uses context, the report should specify what context was available, whether it could leak the answer, and how terminology or named-entity gains were evaluated. If it performs normalization, the report should distinguish formatting improvement from recognition improvement.

These methods introduce a shared set of risks. AVSR introduces modality mismatch, missing-modality behavior, synchronization failures, privacy concerns, and visual-domain bias. Speech-text and SpeechLM systems can blur the boundary between transcription, translation, understanding, and generation. LLM-assisted systems can hallucinate, over-correct, leak context, hide uncertainty, or bias output toward high-resource language norms. All three families can increase compute opacity and reduce reproducibility if model versions, prompts, data sources, adapters, decoding constraints, and inference costs are not reported. These are not reasons to exclude the methods; they are reasons to evaluate them under the same reliability framework used for data, adaptation, and pseudo-labeling.

For low-resource ASR, system choice should follow the enabling condition. Noisy or far-field audio with reliable visual streams may justify AVSR, but only with audio-only, visual-only where meaningful, and fused results under clean and degraded conditions. Multilingual transfer needs may justify speech-text foundation models, but ASR, translation, and correction evidence should remain separate. Orthographic uncertainty should first trigger transcript-policy and scoring controls before LLM correction is used. Domain terminology may justify contextual biasing or constrained correction, but leakage and hallucination must be tested. Compute-limited deployment may favor smaller adapted or distilled ASR systems over large multimodal or external LLM calls unless cost is reported and justified.

This separation between established evidence and emerging directions is essential for the review's field-level argument. AVSR has clearer evidence as a robustness technique under degraded audio than as a broad low-resource language-coverage solution. Speech-text foundation models provide important transfer and multitask context, but ASR-specific claims still require transcript-focused evaluation. LLM-assisted correction and contextualization remain promising mainly when constrained by ASR hypotheses, validated context, or explicit normalization rules. Without those boundaries, newer systems can obscure rather than solve low-resource reliability problems.

Multimodal and LLM-assisted ASR should therefore be visible in the review, but not treated as solved solutions. Their value depends on available modalities, language coverage, data quality, task definition, adaptation method, supervision source, evaluation protocol, reproducibility, compute, privacy, and source grounding. Section 12 synthesizes these directions with the resource, language, model, adaptation, supervision, and evaluation layers developed above.

## 12. Cross-Block Synthesis and Gap Analysis

### 12.1 Foundation Models Shift the Bottleneck

The sections above support one central synthesis: in the foundation-model era, low-resource ASR is no longer defined only by whether a recognizer can be trained from a small labeled corpus. Earlier work on under-resourced speech recognition already treated data scarcity, linguistic-resource scarcity, corpus design, and evaluation as linked problems [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey; @gales2014_babel_project_research_cued]. Self-supervised, multilingual, weakly supervised, and speech-text models now make it possible to begin from representations trained on far more speech than a single low-resource project could collect [@baevski2020wav2vec2; @babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms; @seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt; @tian25b_interspeech]. That change is substantial, but it does not remove the low-resource condition.

The bottleneck has shifted from "can a model be trained at all?" to "can the model be documented, adapted, evaluated, reproduced, and deployed for the target language condition?" Across Sections 3-11, labeled hours, validation coverage, unlabeled data quality, dialect balance, orthographic policy, domain mismatch, compute, adaptation, supervision, robustness evaluation, AVSR, SpeechLMs, and LLM-assisted ASR all point to the same pattern: model scale broadens the feasible strategy set while increasing the need for explicit reliability controls.

This synthesis prevents two weak interpretations: the pre-foundation-model view that low-resource ASR is mainly a supervised-data problem, and the opposite view that large multilingual models have made it a solved transfer problem. The evidence reviewed here supports neither extreme. Large pretrained models may reduce cold-start difficulty, but the final system still depends on target-language validation, normalization decisions, domain and channel fit, adaptation design, pseudo-label quality, subgroup evaluation, reproducibility, and practical deployment constraints.

### 12.2 Cross-Layer Interactions

The taxonomy introduced earlier is useful only if the layers are read as interacting conditions rather than separate boxes. Resource conditions shape language conditions because the available data often over-represent particular speakers, dialects, domains, or writing practices. Community and benchmark datasets expand access, but they also make documentation, sociolinguistic coverage, and data-quality checks part of the ASR problem rather than external metadata tasks [@ardila2020commonvoice; @conneau2022fleurs; @lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech]. If the corpus lacks dialect labels, transcript-policy notes, channel information, or validation splits, the model result can look stronger than the deployable system actually is.

Resource and language conditions also shape model choice. A multilingual foundation model may provide useful cross-lingual structure, but its value depends on language coverage, pretraining provenance, script representation, acoustic match, and task definition. Whisper, XLS-R, MMS, Seamless, and OpusLM show that foundation models can change what is possible, especially for multilingual and speech-text settings [@babu22_interspeech; @pmlr-v202-radford23a; @pratap2024mms; @seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt; @tian25b_interspeech]. Yet these models should not be compared only by headline average WER or language count. Their usefulness for a specific low-resource setting depends on whether the test condition matches the target language variety, channel, domain, orthography, and deployment budget.

Adaptation is the next interaction point. Fine-tuning, parameter-efficient adaptation, continued pretraining, adapters, LoRA-style methods, and quantized adaptation all offer different trade-offs in trainable parameters, compute, stability, and reproducibility [@pmlr-v97-houlsby19a; @hu2022lora; @dettmers2023qlora; @udupa24_interspeech; @qian24_interspeech]. But adaptation cannot be evaluated independently of data quality. A method that appears efficient on a clean benchmark may be less reliable when the transcripts are inconsistent, the dialect distribution is skewed, the validation set is too small, or the test set is closer to the pretraining distribution than to the intended deployment domain.

Supervision strategies add another layer of dependency. Pseudo-labeling, self-training, distillation, and multi-teacher pipelines can increase the amount of usable supervision, but they also introduce teacher bias, confidence-calibration errors, and disagreement between acoustic and language-model evidence [@park20d_interspeech; @khurana2020dust; @leal21_interspeech; @bhogale24_interspeech]. Pseudo-labels are therefore not simply "free transcripts." They are weak supervision whose value depends on filtering, disagreement analysis, held-out validation, and error auditing. In low-resource ASR, a pseudo-labeling pipeline that improves pooled WER can still damage minority dialects, rare names, code-switched segments, or nonstandard orthographic forms.

Evaluation closes the loop. Benchmarks such as ML-SUPERB and AfriSpeech-200 make multilingual and accent-sensitive evaluation more visible, while work on demographic and hallucination risks shows why aggregate error rates are insufficient for reliability claims [@shi2023mlsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200; @koenecke2020racialdisparities; @koenecke2024carelesswhisper]. The same logic applies to multimodal and LLM-assisted extensions. AVSR may help when audio is degraded and visual streams are reliable, but it requires modality-specific validation [@shi2022avhubert; @anwar23_interspeech]. LLM-assisted correction or rescoring may help under constrained conditions, but it must be evaluated for source grounding, hallucination, over-correction, leakage, and dialect or language bias. These evaluation requirements are not afterthoughts; they feed back into how data should be collected, how adaptation should be compared, and how future systems should be reported.

### 12.3 Persistent Gaps

The first persistent gap is definitional and documentary. Many studies still describe languages or settings as low-resource without specifying the relevant dimensions: labeled hours, validation quality, unlabeled speech availability, dialect coverage, orthographic stability, channel, domain, compute budget, and deployment context. This makes it hard to compare results across studies, because "low-resource" can mean a small supervised corpus, weak benchmark coverage, missing transcript policy, scarce compute, or a mismatch between training and deployment. A stronger review and reporting norm would define the resource condition explicitly and separate data volume from data quality.

The second gap concerns comparability across foundation models and adaptation methods. Average results across many languages are useful for broad orientation, but they can hide the settings where a model fails. Similarly, adaptation papers often vary the base model, data size, training recipe, decoding procedure, validation split, and compute budget at the same time. Without matched baselines and subgroup reporting, it is difficult to know whether gains come from the adaptation method, the pretrained model, the corpus, the scoring convention, or the evaluation split. Parameter-efficient methods are especially important for low-resource work because they may reduce the cost of adaptation, but their ASR-specific evidence must still be tied to language coverage, robustness, and reproducibility rather than only to parameter counts.

The third gap is supervision reliability. Pseudo-labeling and distillation are attractive because they expand supervision when verified transcripts are limited. The unresolved problem is how to tell when the added supervision is trustworthy. Confidence thresholds alone may be brittle; teacher agreement can help but may also amplify shared biases; and language-model-heavy teachers may normalize away exactly the dialect, code-switching, names, or orthographic forms that a low-resource system needs to preserve. Future studies need clearer reporting of teacher identity, filtering criteria, disagreement patterns, error types, and held-out validation conditions.

The fourth gap is evaluation and robustness. Pooled WER and CER remain necessary, but they are insufficient when the central risks are dialect imbalance, demographic disparity, domain mismatch, orthographic inconsistency, hallucination, code-switching, and compute-constrained deployment. Evaluation should report what was normalized, which subgroups or domains were tested, whether the system was evaluated outside the training-like condition, and whether improvements remain under noisy audio, scarce validation, or constrained inference. This is also where AVSR, SpeechLMs, and LLM-assisted ASR must be treated carefully. They are important future-facing directions, but they should not be used to make broad low-resource claims unless the evaluation separates transcription from translation or correction, tests missing or degraded inputs where relevant, and reports reproducibility and cost.

### 12.4 Synthesis for the Future Agenda

The cross-block synthesis turns the review from a catalog of methods into a reliability argument. Low-resource ASR should be analyzed as an interaction among resource conditions, language conditions, model families, adaptation methods, supervision sources, and evaluation protocols. Foundation models matter because they change the feasible starting point and expand the set of transfer, adaptation, and speech-text strategies. They do not, by themselves, guarantee reliable ASR for the languages and communities that remain under-documented, dialectally diverse, orthographically unstable, domain-mismatched, or compute-constrained.

This synthesis also clarifies the contribution of the review. The field needs more than another ranking of model families. It needs a vocabulary for defining low-resource conditions, a way to connect data and language properties to adaptation choices, a framework for treating pseudo-labels and distillation as reliability problems, and evaluation practices that expose subgroup, domain, channel, normalization, hallucination, and deployment risks. Section 13 therefore develops the future agenda from these gaps rather than from a single preferred method. The agenda should ask how low-resource ASR can become more documented, comparable, robust, reproducible, and useful under real constraints.

## 13. Future Research Agenda

### 13.1 Reliability as the Future Agenda

The synthesis in Section 12 leads to a future agenda organized around reliability rather than model scale alone. The remaining problem is not simply how to train larger multilingual systems, but how to make low-resource ASR claims interpretable, comparable, reproducible, deployable, and robust for underrepresented languages and speech communities. Future progress should therefore be judged by whether studies make the full evidence chain visible: the resource condition, language condition, model choice, adaptation path, supervision source, evaluation protocol, compute cost, and deployment context.

This agenda follows directly from the review's main question. Future studies should specify which low-resource condition they address, report how transfer is controlled and where it fails, and add safeguards when AVSR, SpeechLMs, or LLM-assisted ASR introduce modality mismatch, task confusion, hallucination, over-correction, leakage, reproducibility, or cost risks.

### 13.2 Define and Document the Low-Resource Condition

The first priority is a stronger definition of the low-resource condition. Future papers should continue to report labeled hours, but they should not stop there. They should describe the quality of labeled speech, the availability of unlabeled speech, whether weakly labeled or web-mined data are used, how validation and test sets are created, which dialects or speaker groups are represented, which domains and channels are covered, how transcripts are normalized, and what compute or deployment constraints apply. Surveys and challenge evidence already support this multidimensional framing of under-resourced ASR [@besacier2014_underresourced_asr_survey; @peterson2022_openasr21]. The next step is to make such reporting standard rather than exceptional.

Dataset documentation should also become part of the evidence base, not a supplementary detail. Resource papers and benchmark reports should identify data provenance, consent and licensing, access conditions, speaker and dialect metadata where ethically appropriate, regional or accent coverage, domain and channel composition, validation protocol, transcript conventions, normalization rules, and scoring scripts. Common Voice, multilingual dataset-quality analysis, and AfriSpeech-200 show that open or broad resources still need careful interpretation through quality, metadata, and benchmark design [@ardila2020commonvoice; @lau2025_data_quality_multilingual_speech; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200]. Reporting frameworks such as datasheets, data statements, model cards, and compute-aware documentation can support this work, provided they are treated as documentation tools rather than ASR performance evidence [@gebru2021_datasheets_for_datasets; @bender2018_data_statements; @mitchell2019_model_cards; @strubell2019_energy_policy].

This documentation agenda should be community-aware. Low-resource ASR often concerns languages for which local speakers, educators, clinicians, broadcasters, or researchers understand variation that cannot be inferred from a web-scale corpus. Metadata should not be collected invasively or mechanically, but the absence of metadata can make failures invisible. Future resource work should therefore combine language expertise, community consent, clear licensing, auditable data statements, and transparent validation.

### 13.3 Build Orthography-Aware, Dialect-Aware, and Domain-Aware Benchmarks

The second priority is benchmark design that exposes language and evaluation risk. Orthography, script, spelling alternatives, punctuation, casing, diacritics, spacing, morphology, and code-switching can all change measured WER or CER. A benchmark that hides tokenization or normalization policy can make two systems look comparable even when their outputs follow different transcript conventions. Lenient and orthography-aware evaluation evidence shows why scoring should account for acceptable variants where the language and task justify it [@karita2023lenient].

Dialect and domain should be treated with the same seriousness. Foundation-model averages can hide whether a system performs well on well-represented varieties while failing on underrepresented dialects, domains, channels, or speaker groups. Future benchmarks should report language-wise, dialect-wise, domain-wise, channel-wise, and pooled results separately where metadata and ethics permit. They should also state whether scores are raw, normalized, macro-averaged, or pooled. FLEURS, ML-SUPERB, and AfriSpeech-200 provide important benchmark anchors, but they also show why language coverage, accent coverage, metadata, and evaluation protocol must be read carefully [@conneau2022fleurs; @shi2023mlsuperb; @olatunji2023_afrispeech200].

The practical benchmark requirement is transparency. Studies should publish normalization rules, scoring scripts, transcript examples, language-specific handling of scripts and morphology, and subgroup breakdowns where possible. Without these controls, a reported gain may reflect formatting compatibility, benchmark familiarity, or majority-variety fit rather than robust recognition improvement.

### 13.4 Compare Adaptation and Supervision Under Real Constraints

The third priority is condition-controlled comparison of adaptation strategies. Full fine-tuning, continued pretraining, adapters, LoRA, QLoRA, prompting, and transfer can all be useful, but none should be promoted as universally best. Future studies should report the base model and checkpoint, adaptation data, trainable parameters, memory, hardware, training cost, inference cost, decoding settings, validation condition, and retention of prior-language performance where relevant. They should compare methods under matched data and compute budgets when possible.

Parameter-efficient methods are attractive for low-resource ASR because they may lower adaptation cost and support modular deployment, but general PEFT evidence should not be substituted for ASR-specific validation [@pmlr-v97-houlsby19a; @hu2022lora; @dettmers2023qlora]. Speech-specific adapter and new-language adaptation studies should carry stronger claims when they evaluate language fit, compute, and forgetting under realistic conditions [@udupa24_interspeech; @qian24_interspeech]. A method that improves accuracy but requires compute unavailable to small labs or language communities has limited practical low-resource value unless that cost is reported and justified.

Pseudo-labeling and knowledge distillation require the same discipline. Future work should treat generated supervision as auditable weak supervision, not as free ground truth. Studies should identify the teacher, teacher training source where known, decoding settings, confidence or uncertainty measures, filtering thresholds, retained-data proportions where possible, normalization policy, label-space compatibility, and validation behavior by language, domain, or dialect. Self-training, Noisy Student, uncertainty-driven filtering, multilingual distillation, and large-scale pseudo-labeling show why teacher quality and filtering cannot remain hidden [@park20d_interspeech; @khurana2020dust; @leal21_interspeech; @bhogale24_interspeech]. Future work should also evaluate pseudo-label damage: dialect bias amplification, over-normalization of valid variants, hallucinated labels, and subgroup losses hidden by aggregate gains.

### 13.5 Evaluate Robustness, Reproducibility, Compute, and Deployment Cost

The fourth priority is evaluation beyond aggregate WER and CER. These metrics remain necessary, but they should be accompanied by language-wise, dialect-wise, domain-wise, channel-wise, and speaker-group reporting where ethically and practically possible. Scores should state tokenization, punctuation, casing, script handling, normalization, averaging, and pooling policy. Robustness protocols should test noise, far-field speech, overlap, conversational speech, and channel shift when such conditions matter for deployment. For generative ASR and LLM-assisted correction, hallucination and unsupported-content checks are needed because fluent output can be wrong in ways that ordinary WER may not expose [@koenecke2024carelesswhisper].

Reproducibility should be reported as a protocol, not as a claim. Future papers should publish scoring scripts, normalization rules, evaluation splits, model versions, decoding settings, prompts or correction constraints where relevant, and artifacts where possible. Toolkits and benchmarks such as SUPERB, ESPnet, SpeechBrain, and Kaldi provide infrastructure for comparison, but their use alone does not prove that a particular low-resource result is reproducible [@yang2021superb; @watanabe2018espnet; @ravanelli2021speechbrain; @povey2011kaldi]. Compute transparency should include model size, trainable parameters, hardware, memory, training cost, inference cost, and deployment assumptions. Without these details, progress may be inaccessible to the communities and labs that low-resource ASR is meant to support.

### 13.6 Bound Multimodal, AVSR, SpeechLM, and LLM-Assisted Directions

Multimodal and LLM-assisted directions should be included in the future agenda, but only under reliability controls. Low-resource AVSR needs multilingual and multidialect resources, clear audio-visual alignment, missing-modality tests, privacy safeguards, and evaluation under degraded audio. AV-HuBERT and MuAViC support the promise of multimodal robustness, but current evidence does not justify treating AVSR as mature for all low-resource settings [@shi2022avhubert; @anwar23_interspeech].

Speech-text and SpeechLM systems also require task-boundary clarity. SeamlessM4T and OpusLM show that ASR, speech translation, text translation, and speech-language modeling are increasingly connected, but transcription, translation, correction, and normalization remain different tasks [@seamless2025_joint_speech_text_mt; @tian25b_interspeech]. Future papers should state what output is being evaluated, whether it is grounded in the source audio, and how the system separates recognition from generation. LLM-assisted correction, rescoring, contextual biasing, and post-ASR normalization should be constrained by N-best lists, lattices, confidence scores, validated context, or explicit normalization rules. Watchlist evidence on LLM correction, hallucination benchmarks, and contextual ASR is useful for defining future evaluation needs, but it should not carry central claims that these methods are reliable across low-resource languages [@ma2024asrerrorcorrection; @koudounas2025shallow; @wang2025contextasrbench].

### 13.7 Reporting Checklist and Takeaway

The agenda can be converted into a practical reporting standard. Future low-resource ASR studies should make visible the language condition, resource condition, model and checkpoint, adaptation method, supervision source, decoding setup, evaluation protocol, robustness tests, reproducibility artifacts, compute cost, deployment assumptions, and safeguards for multimodal or LLM-assisted components. This checklist is intentionally broad because the low-resource condition is broad. It is not a demand that every study solve every problem; it is a demand that every study make its evidence chain inspectable.

## 14. Conclusion

### 14.1 Answering the Review Question

This review asked how foundation speech models have changed low-resource ASR, and what resource, adaptation, evaluation, and robustness challenges still prevent reliable recognition for underrepresented languages. The answer is that strong pretrained representations, multilingual transfer, weakly supervised training, and broader speech-language model families now give many low-resource ASR studies a better initial baseline than earlier systems could provide. The field has moved beyond the assumption that every underrepresented language must begin from a small, isolated, task-specific recognizer.

That shift does not make low-resource ASR a solved problem. Reliable recognition still depends on the quality and provenance of labeled, unlabeled, weakly labeled, and pseudo-labeled data; the fit between pretraining mixtures and target-language conditions; the handling of dialect, orthography, domain, channel, script, and code-switching; the choice of adaptation strategy under realistic compute and forgetting constraints; the reliability of pseudo-labels and teacher models; and evaluation protocols that expose subgroup, domain, robustness, hallucination, and reproducibility risks. Foundation models therefore move the bottleneck from whether a model can be trained at all toward whether a system can be trusted, adapted, evaluated, reproduced, and deployed under a clearly specified low-resource condition.

### 14.2 Contributions and Implications

The review makes four connected contributions. First, it reframes low-resource ASR through a foundation-model-era taxonomy that treats low-resource status as a multidimensional condition rather than a shortage of transcribed hours. The taxonomy links resources, language conditions, model families, adaptation methods, supervision strategies, and evaluation settings so that low-resource claims can be interpreted more precisely. Second, the review synthesizes data-centric and model-centric solutions, showing that corpus quality, validation, metadata, normalization, filtering, augmentation, self-supervised learning, multilingual transfer, and weakly supervised foundation models interact. Larger or more multilingual models can improve coverage, but their behavior still depends on the data and evaluation conditions through which they are trained and assessed.

Third, the review critically examines adaptation, pseudo-labeling, and knowledge distillation as conditional tools. Fine-tuning, continued pretraining, adapters, LoRA-style methods, pseudo-labeling, self-training, and single- or multi-teacher distillation can all be useful, but none is universally reliable. Their value depends on target-language evidence, domain fit, compute budget, teacher quality, confidence filtering, teacher disagreement, label-space compatibility, and evaluation design. Fourth, the review develops a future research agenda for reliable, multimodal, and LLM-assisted low-resource ASR. That agenda calls for clearer low-resource definitions, better dataset documentation, orthography-aware and dialect-aware benchmarks, compute-efficient and forgetting-aware adaptation, auditable pseudo-labeling and distillation, evaluation beyond aggregate WER/CER, reproducibility and compute transparency, multimodal robustness where justified, and bounded LLM-assisted correction or rescoring under source-grounded evaluation.

Together, these contributions support the review's main claim: progress in low-resource ASR should be judged by whether systems become more reliable under real resource, language, and deployment conditions, not only by whether they improve pooled benchmark scores. Aggregate WER and CER remain useful, but they are insufficient when used alone. A system can improve a pooled score while still failing on a dialect, domain, channel, orthographic convention, speaker group, noisy condition, or evaluation protocol that matters for the target community. A system can also appear fluent while introducing unsupported content, over-correction, or context-driven rewriting in LLM-assisted settings.

The field should therefore move from a simple "larger model plus pooled WER" logic toward transparent evidence chains. Such chains should report what resource setting is being studied, how data were collected and validated, which language and dialect conditions are represented, how transcripts and normalization were handled, what model and checkpoint were used, how adaptation was performed, how supervision was expanded or filtered, how evaluation was broken down, and what robustness, compute, reproducibility, privacy, and deployment constraints apply. Without these details, it remains difficult to know whether a reported gain reflects genuine recognition improvement, benchmark compatibility, data leakage, formatting choices, teacher-model artifacts, or unreported compute scale.

### 14.3 Closing Statement

This reliability framing also clarifies how emerging directions should be interpreted. AVSR, multimodal modeling, SpeechLMs, and LLM-assisted correction or rescoring are important research directions, especially for noisy speech, contextual terminology, and multimodal deployment settings. But they should be treated as reliability-sensitive extensions rather than universal solutions. They require task-boundary clarity, modality-coverage checks, missing-modality evaluation, hallucination and over-correction tests, source grounding, reproducibility, and compute reporting. In low-resource ASR, adding another modality or a language model is only progress when it makes the resulting system more trustworthy under the target condition.

Foundation speech models have altered low-resource ASR by making stronger pretrained systems and broader multilingual baselines available. Yet the central challenge for underrepresented languages remains reliability: building, adapting, evaluating, reproducing, and deploying ASR systems whose behavior is understood under specific resource, language, domain, supervision, and robustness conditions. Foundation models are not the end of low-resource ASR; they are the new starting point for making it more reliable.
